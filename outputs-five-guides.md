## What assisted living actually costs in Evanston and Illinois

Short answer

The median cost of assisted living in Illinois is **$6,219 a month**, according to the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey. That number is a starting point, not a quote. What a family actually pays depends on the level of care assessed, whether a second person is living in the apartment, and a one-time community fee that the monthly figure never includes.

### What the median actually measures

A median is the middle of a range. Half of Illinois communities charge more than $6,219 a month and half charge less. It is a statewide figure, not an Evanston figure. CareScout publishes no separate median for Evanston or for the north-shore suburbs, so this guide will not tell you how local prices compare — it does not know. Treat the statewide median as the number that tells you whether a quote is roughly normal, not as the number you will pay.

For comparison, the same survey puts the Illinois nursing home median at **$8,304 a month** for a semi-private room and **$9,216** for a private room. Those are different levels of care, not different prices for the same thing.

There is no Illinois median published for independent living, and this guide does not invent one. Where a reliable figure does not exist, saying so is more useful than filling the gap.

### The four costs that surprise families

**A community fee.** A one-time charge at move-in, separate from rent. Ask for the amount in writing and ask whether any part of it is refundable if your parent leaves within a few months.

**Care levels.** Most communities assess a resident and assign a care tier that is billed on top of the base rate. The tier can change after a fall, a hospital stay, or a medication change. Ask what each tier costs, what moves someone between tiers, and who decides.

**A second person.** If both parents move in, there is almost always a second-occupant fee. It is rarely on the brochure.

**The annual increase.** Ask what the rate rose by in each of the last three years. A community that cannot answer that question has told you something.

### What this means in Evanston

A statewide median cannot tell you what any one Evanston community charges, and the spread between communities is wider than a single number suggests. Rather than guess at a local average, get the actual numbers from the actual buildings — there are not many of them, and they will quote you.

The practical move is to collect three written quotes that each itemise base rent, the current care tier, the community fee and the second-person fee. Comparing all-in monthly totals is the only comparison that means anything.

### If paying privately is not realistic

Illinois runs a Medicaid programme that pays for care in approved assisted-living-style settings, called the Supportive Living Program. It is not the same as private-pay assisted living and not every community participates. It is explained in detail in Illinois Supportive Living Program, explained.

Medicare does not pay for assisted living. That surprises most families, and the reason is worth understanding before you plan around it — see Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?

## Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?

Short answer

**Medicare does not pay for assisted living**, and it does not pay for **long-term custodial** nursing home care. It pays only for short-term skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospital stay: days 1–20 cost **$0**, days 21–100 cost **$217 each day** in 2026, and from day 101 you pay everything.

### The distinction that decides everything

Medicare divides care into skilled and custodial, and that single distinction decides whether it pays anything at all.

**Skilled care** is nursing or therapy that, in Medicare's own words, can only be safely and effectively performed by or under the supervision of licensed professionals — intravenous medication, wound care, physical therapy after a fracture. **Custodial care** is help with bathing, dressing, eating, getting to the toilet, medication reminders and supervision.

Medicare pays for skilled care, short-term, under conditions listed below. It pays **nothing** toward custodial care, however much of it someone needs and however long they paid into the system. Assisted living is custodial care. That is the whole reason Medicare does not cover it.

**It is not the building that decides this — it is the stay.** The same nursing home room can be covered one month and not the next. A qualifying short-term rehabilitation stay in a Medicare-certified facility is covered. If that same resident stays on in that same bed because they can no longer live alone, the stay has become long-term custodial care and Medicare pays none of it. Families who were told "Medicare is covering mom’s nursing home" are almost always describing the first situation and planning for the second.

### The five conditions, all of which must hold

Medicare requires every one of these for a skilled nursing stay to be covered. One missing condition is enough to make the whole stay private-pay:

 - A **qualifying inpatient hospital stay** — at least 3 days in a row, counting from the day of inpatient admission and not counting the day of discharge

 - Admission to the facility **generally within 30 days** of leaving the hospital

 - A doctor’s decision that **daily skilled care** is needed

 - The care given in a **Medicare-certified** skilled nursing facility

 - Skilled services for a condition treated during that hospital stay, or one that arose while being treated for it

**The trap inside the first condition.** Hours spent under observation or in the emergency room do not count toward the three days, even if your parent was in a hospital bed overnight. A family can spend four days in a hospital and have zero qualifying days. Ask, out loud, on the first day: has my parent been admitted as an inpatient, or are they under observation? Then ask again, because the status can be changed.

Two exceptions exist. A doctor in an Accountable Care Organization with a Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver may not need the three days, and Medicare Advantage plans may waive it too. Ask the hospital and ask the plan — neither will volunteer it.

### What a covered skilled nursing stay costs in 2026

These are the costs of a qualifying skilled stay only. Nothing below applies to long-term custodial care, assisted living or memory care, where the Medicare share is zero. On a covered stay, Part A pays on this schedule:

 - **Days 1–20:** $0 each day, after the Part A deductible of $1,736 per benefit period

 - **Days 21–100:** $217 each day

 - **Day 101 and beyond:** you pay all costs

You do not pay the Part A deductible twice. If it was already paid during the hospital stay in the same benefit period, it is not charged again for the skilled nursing stay.

Run the arithmetic before you rely on the 100 days. A full run of days 21 to 100 is **$17,360 out of pocket**, and coverage stops entirely at day 101.

If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan rather than Original Medicare, the rules differ — some plans charge copayments during the first 20 days. Ask the plan directly, in writing.

### The word that ends coverage early

Coverage can stop before day 100. If the facility determines that a resident is no longer improving, benefits can end on the grounds that skilled care is no longer needed.

Families are rarely told this will happen until it does. Ask the discharge planner on day one what the expected length of the covered stay is, and ask to be told in writing as soon as that assessment changes. That notice is what gives you time to plan rather than days.

### So who does pay for assisted living?

Three realistic sources. Private funds, including savings, pension income and the proceeds of a house. A long-term care insurance policy, if one was bought years ago. Or Illinois Medicaid, through the Supportive Living Program, which does pay for care in approved settings.

For what private payment actually costs in this state, see What assisted living actually costs in Evanston and Illinois.

## Illinois Supportive Living Program, explained

Short answer

The Supportive Living Program is how Illinois Medicaid pays for assisted-living-style care. It covers the **cost of services** in an approved Supportive Living facility. The resident pays a state-set room and board charge, plus a **group care credit** calculated from their own income, and keeps a personal needs allowance of **$120 a month**.

### What it is, and what it is not

A Supportive Living facility is not the same thing as a private-pay assisted living community, and it is not a nursing home. It is a separate category of setting, approved by the state, where Medicaid pays for the care a resident receives.

Not every assisted living community in Illinois participates. A community can be excellent and still not be a Supportive Living facility. Ask directly, early, because it decides whether a place is financially possible at all.

### How the money actually works

**Expect two charges, not one.** The first is room and board, which stays the resident's responsibility. Illinois sets it statewide and adjusts it each year with the Social Security cost-of-living increase: **$874 a month** for single occupancy and **$625.50** for double occupancy, effective 1 January 2026.

The second is the **group care credit**, also called patient liability. It is not a fixed price and it is not the same for two residents of the same building. The state calculates it from that resident's own countable income after allowable deductions — health insurance premiums, certain medical expenses, the room and board portion, and the $120 personal needs allowance. Whatever remains is paid monthly toward the cost of care, and Medicaid covers the rest.

So there is no sum on this page that produces your parent's figure, and any site that offers you one is guessing. The amount owed is determined by the state for that individual, from documents you have not filed yet. What you can know in advance is the method, the two charges, and the one thing that is fixed: the **$120 personal needs allowance** a resident keeps for everything personal — clothing, haircuts, a phone, gifts for grandchildren. Most residents owe both charges; a resident avoids the group care credit only when their income equals the SSI amount.

### Questions worth asking before you apply

 - Is this specific community an approved Supportive Living facility?

 - Is there a waiting list, and how long is it in practice?

 - What is the room and board amount at this facility?

 - What happens if my parent's needs exceed what this setting provides?

 - Who at the facility helps with the application, and what do we need to gather first?

Eligibility rules, income tests and asset limits change, and they are decided by the state rather than by any community. Confirm current figures with Illinois HFS or a benefits counsellor before making any financial decision. This guide explains how the programme works; it is not an eligibility determination.

### How this fits the rest of the picture

If you are comparing this against paying privately, What assisted living actually costs in Evanston and Illinois has the private-pay medians.

If you are hoping Medicare will cover some of it, read Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care? first. It does not, and planning around the belief that it might costs families months.

## How to check an Illinois care community's inspection record

Short answer

Two free public tools. The **Illinois Department of Public Health Health Care Report Card** publishes inspection and complaint information for licensed Illinois facilities. **Medicare Care Compare** publishes staffing levels, inspection findings and quality measures for Medicare-certified nursing homes. Check both before you tour anywhere.

### Start with the state

The Illinois Department of Public Health licenses and inspects long-term care facilities in this state and publishes what it finds. Search the Health Care Report Card by facility name or location.

What you are looking for is not a perfect record — almost nobody has one. You are looking for patterns. A single citation three years ago that was corrected is noise. The same category of problem appearing in consecutive inspections is a signal.

### Then check Medicare Care Compare

Medicare Care Compare covers Medicare-certified nursing homes and publishes something the state tool does not emphasise: reported staffing levels, including registered nurse hours per resident per day, and staff turnover.

Staffing is the measure most closely tied to daily experience. A community with high turnover is one where your parent meets a new caregiver every few weeks, and where nobody knows their history without reading a chart.

Note the scope limit honestly: Care Compare covers nursing homes. Assisted living is licensed by the state rather than Medicare-certified, so for assisted living the Illinois tool is the one that applies.

### Reading a record without panicking

 - **Check the date.** A finding from four years ago under different management is weaker evidence than one from last quarter.

 - **Check severity.** Citations are scaled. A paperwork deficiency is not a resident-harm finding.

 - **Check whether it recurred.** Correction is normal. Repetition is the problem.

 - **Take it with you.** Print the record and ask the community about a specific finding on your visit. How they answer tells you as much as the finding did.

### Check every community, including ours

This guide is written by someone who works at Westminster Place. That is exactly why this page exists and why it names the tools plainly: **look Westminster Place up too.** Run the same searches, read the same records, and ask the same questions there that you would ask anywhere else.

A resource that tells you how to check everyone except the author's own employer would not be worth reading.

For what to look at with your own eyes once you are inside a building, the free six-question assessment ends with observations and questions specific to the level of care your parent may need.

## Assisted living, memory care, skilled nursing and rehabilitation — what is the difference?

Short answer

**Assisted living** is daily help with bathing, dressing and medication, in a resident's own apartment. **Memory care** is a secured setting with dementia-trained staff, where the question is safety rather than help. **Skilled nursing** is licensed nursing care around the clock. **Short-term rehabilitation** is therapy after a hospital stay, measured in weeks, with going home as the goal.

### Assisted living

Hands-on help with the things that happen every day — bathing, dressing, medications — while someone keeps their own apartment and as much independence as possible. Services vary enormously between communities, which is why two places with the same label can be very different.

Illinois median: **$6,219 a month**. Medicare does not cover it.

### Memory care

Structured support for significant memory or cognitive change, usually in a secured area with staff trained in dementia care. The deciding question is not how much help someone needs but whether they are safe — wandering, leaving the stove on, not recognising where they are.

CareScout does not publish a separate Illinois memory care median, so this guide does not state one. In practice it is generally priced above assisted living in the same community; ask for the specific figure in writing.

### Skilled nursing

Licensed nursing available 24 hours a day, for medical and physical needs beyond what assisted living provides. A medical setting — usually a room rather than an apartment.

Illinois median: **$8,304 a month** semi-private, **$9,216** private. Medicare covers a short skilled stay after a qualifying hospital admission, not long-term residence.

### Short-term rehabilitation

Physical, occupational and speech therapy after illness, surgery or a hospital stay. A stay measured in weeks, with going home as the goal. It is delivered in a skilled nursing setting, which is why the two get confused.

This is the one Medicare may cover, under conditions. The schedule and the limits are set out in Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?

### How to tell which one applies

The honest answer is that it depends on specifics — what someone can still do alone, what has changed recently, whether there has been a hospital stay, and whether safety is the concern rather than help.

The free six-question assessment on this site walks through those specifics and shows which level the answers point to, along with the reasoning and the exact answers that produced it. It asks for no name, no email and no phone number, and nothing you enter leaves your device.

It is an educational starting point, not a diagnosis. A doctor, a hospital discharge planner or a geriatric care manager can assess things no website can.
