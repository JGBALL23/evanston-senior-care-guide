Moving a parent to Illinois from another state: what happens to Medicare and Medicaid?

Short answer

Original Medicare moves with your parent; another state’s Medicaid does not, and Illinois Medicaid needs its own application. Medicare.gov says that with Original Medicare your parent can use “any doctor or hospital that takes Medicare, anywhere in the U.S.” A Medicare Advantage Plan or a Medicare drug plan is tied to a service area: after a move out of it, Medicare.gov says “Your chance to switch begins when you move and continues for 2 full months after you move.” Illinois HFS says “Medicaid provides health coverage for Illinois residents who meet certain eligibility criteria including income limits and immigration status.” If your parent will need the state to pay for a nursing home, HFS says they must apply for medical benefits through the Department of Human Services and obtain a needs screening.

Medicare goes with your parent

Medicare.gov’s comparison of the two ways to get Medicare says that with Original Medicare “You can use any doctor or hospital that takes Medicare, anywhere in the U.S.” It adds: “You may pay more if your doctor doesn’t accept assignment.” A Medicare Advantage Plan works differently: the same page says “You may need to use doctors and other providers who are in the plan’s network and service area” for non-emergency care.

Your parent’s address with Medicare is kept by Social Security. Medicare.gov says: “To change your official address with Medicare, you have to contact Social Security (SSA).” It adds that this applies “even if you don’t get Social Security benefits”.

Sorting out coverage for a move is a different question from knowing what kind of care your parent will need once they are here.

A Medicare Advantage or drug plan: the two-month window

If your parent has a Medicare Advantage Plan or a stand-alone Medicare drug plan, check whether the plan serves the place in Illinois they are moving to. Medicare.gov’s page on Special Enrollment Periods covers two cases. If the new address is not in the plan’s service area, your parent can switch to a new Medicare Advantage Plan or Medicare drug plan, and a parent in a Medicare Advantage Plan can also go back to Original Medicare. If the new address is still in the plan’s service area but there are new plan options there, your parent can switch to a new plan too.

The timing is the same in both cases: “Your chance to switch begins when you move and continues for 2 full months after you move.” Telling the plan early gives a little more room: “If you tell your plan before you move, your chance to switch plans begins the month before the month you move and continues for 2 full months after you move.”

Doing nothing has a consequence for a parent in a Medicare Advantage Plan who moves out of its area: “If you move outside your old plan’s service area and don’t join a new Medicare Advantage Plan during this Special Enrollment Period, you’ll be enrolled in Original Medicare when you’re dropped from your old Medicare Advantage Plan.”

A move into a nursing home has its own window on the same page. For someone who lives in, or recently moved out of, an institution such as a nursing home or rehabilitation hospital, Medicare.gov says: “Your chance to join, switch, or drop coverage lasts as long as you live in the institution and for 2 full months after the month you move out of the institution.”

Questions can go to 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227; TTY 1-877-486-2048), the number the Special Enrollment Periods page gives. Once your parent lives in Illinois, the guide to SHIP, Illinois’s free Medicare counselling explains where to get unbiased help comparing plans.

A Medigap policy

For a parent with Original Medicare and a Medigap (Medicare Supplement) policy who is moving out of state, Medicare.gov’s page on switching or dropping a Medigap policy says: “You can keep your current Medigap policy no matter where you live, as long as you still have Original Medicare.” Switching to another policy is a different matter: the same page warns that your parent “may have to pay more for your new Medigap policy and answer some medical questions if you decide to switch your Medigap policy outside of your Medigap Open Enrollment Period.” A Medicare SELECT policy has its own options on that page, and Medicare.gov says the answers cover federal rights only, so it points readers to their State Insurance Department about rights under state law.

A parent who leaves a Medicare Advantage Plan for Original Medicare because of the move has a federal right to buy a Medigap policy. Medicare.gov’s guide Choosing a Medigap Policy lists, among the cases in which someone may buy a policy outside the Medigap Open Enrollment Period, a parent with a Medicare Advantage Plan who moves out of the plan’s service area, and says: “You only have this right if you switch to Original Medicare (rather than join another Medicare Advantage Plan).” The right covers certain Medigap plans sold by an insurance company in your parent’s state. The guide gives the timing as 60 days before the date the Medicare Advantage Plan coverage ends, if your parent is choosing to leave the plan, and no more than 63 days after that coverage ends, and adds: “Medigap policies can’t start until your Medicare Advantage Plan coverage ends.”

Medicaid does not move: Illinois needs its own application

HFS describes Medicaid as the health coverage programmes managed by the State of Illinois, and says: “Medicaid provides health coverage for Illinois residents who meet certain eligibility criteria including income limits and immigration status.” For an older parent, the HFS coverage group is Aid to the Aged Blind or Disabled: “Aid to the Aged Blind or Disabled (AABD) provides health coverage to people who are age 65 or older, or are blind, or have a disability.” HFS suggests a quick screening first, which asks for basic information about your household and income, but adds: “Even if the screening tool says you may qualify for Medicaid, you must still complete a full application to apply.” The same page says: “There is no penalty for applying, even if you are not found eligible.”

How long must your parent have lived in Illinois? HFS’s question-and-answer sheet on Medicaid applications for long-term care, dated 6 November 2014, answers that question for a nursing home: “An individual in a nursing home is considered a resident of Illinois unless they are placed in an Illinois facility and covered by another state’s Medicaid program.” The same sheet says the Department of Human Services can verify Illinois residence electronically for people who have Social Security numbers. This guide found no HFS page that sets a minimum length of residence for other settings, and none that explains how to end coverage in the state your parent is leaving: ask that state’s Medicaid agency, and ask Illinois when you apply.

Help with Medicare costs works the same way. Medicare.gov says of the Medicare Savings Programs: “You’ll apply for Medicare Savings Programs through your state.” A parent who had one of these programs in another state applies in Illinois; the guide to Illinois’s Medicare Savings Programs sets out the Illinois limits and how to apply.

If your parent is moving into a nursing home

HFS’s long-term care page says: “If you want the state to pay for your nursing home care, you must:” apply for medical benefits through the Department of Human Services (DHS), and obtain a needs screening through the Department on Aging (DoA) or the Department of Human Services (DHS). The guide to Illinois’s nursing home pre-admission screening explains the screening, and the guide to how Illinois Medicaid pays for long-term care covers the income and asset rules, including what a spouse who stays at home may keep. The guide to the look-back period explains how gifts and transfers made before an application are treated.

Illinois also has a Medicaid-funded alternative to a nursing home for some people. HFS says its Supportive Living Program offers individuals age 65 and older, or individuals with physical disabilities ages 22 to 64, services to remain in a community setting; the guide to the Supportive Living Program explains how it works.
