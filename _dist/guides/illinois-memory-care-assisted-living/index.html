What does Illinois require of memory care in an assisted living community?

Short answer

Memory care in an Illinois assisted living community is licensed and surveyed by the state, not by Medicare. The Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) calls assisted living “a state licensure program with no federal oversight”. Its 2026–2029 Alzheimer’s plan says staff serving people with dementia in an assisted living facility providing Alzheimer’s or dementia services must take “six hours of initial dementia training and three hours of annual dementia training thereafter”, and that settings offering specialised dementia care must disclose information about their program to the agency that licenses them. Those are the facts you can check before a tour.

Who licenses memory care in Illinois assisted living

IDPH’s Division of Assisted Living is “responsible for all state licensure and survey processes for Assisted Living and Shared Housing establishments.” Every establishment must send IDPH an annual renewal application with its licence fee.

IDPH’s Alzheimer’s plan describes the same division as conducting annual and complaint surveys, incident report investigations and follow-up surveys, and adds why there is no federal oversight: “This is a state licensure program with no federal oversight, as the residents in both assisted living and memory care areas are private pay through an establishment contract.”

The plan is also plain about the rules themselves: “There are basic dementia provisions in the regulations for ALFs”. The more detailed Special Care Unit regulations it names apply to skilled nursing and intermediate care facilities, not to assisted living. This guide does not summarise the assisted living dementia provisions themselves, because their text is published outside the state agency pages this library cites; IDPH’s Assisted Living page links to it under Laws & Rules.

The training staff must have

According to IDPH’s plan, staff serving people with Alzheimer’s disease or a related dementia in a skilled nursing facility, assisted living facility, home health provider or hospice program that provides Alzheimer’s or dementia services are required to take “six hours of initial dementia training and three hours of annual dementia training thereafter.”

The person in charge of the dementia program has a higher bar: “Alzheimer’s services supervisors in skilled nursing facilities and assisted living facilities are required to take 12 hours of annual dementia training”. The plan adds that the training topics are prescribed in statute and associated rules.

The program disclosure

IDPH’s plan states that Illinois has an Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias Special Care Disclosure Act that “requires licensed residential care settings that provide specialized care to individuals with dementia to disclose information about their program to the state agency responsible for licensing of that setting”. A licensed community that provides specialised dementia care is, in other words, required to have disclosed information about its program to the state.

Questions to ask on a memory care tour

Each of these follows from a rule above, so a community should be able to answer it plainly:

  • Is this memory care area part of a licence from IDPH’s Division of Assisted Living, and under what name is it listed?
  • May we see the description of your dementia program that you disclosed to the state?
  • How many hours of dementia training have the staff who would care for my parent completed, and when was their last annual training?
  • Who is your Alzheimer’s services supervisor, and how is their annual training kept up to date?
  • What does the establishment contract say my parent will pay, and what changes the price?

If private pay is not possible, and when to plan

Because assisted living memory care is private pay through an establishment contract, one option for a family that cannot pay privately is Illinois’ Supportive Living Program, which IDPH’s plan describes as an alternative to nursing home care for low-income older people, paid through a Medicaid waiver obtained by the Department of Healthcare and Family Services, with the resident still paying room and board. IDPH’s plan says “There are currently 20 SLP dementia care settings with approximately 520 apartments.” See Illinois Supportive Living Program, explained for how it works.

The same plan is candid that supply is short: “the availability of even costly assisted living facilities (ALFs) with dementia care does not meet the demand.” If memory care is likely, it is worth visiting communities early.
