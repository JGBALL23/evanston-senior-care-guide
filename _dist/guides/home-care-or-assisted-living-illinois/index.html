Home care or assisted living for a parent in Illinois: what each costs and what pays for it

Short answer

It depends mostly on how many hours of help your parent needs, and whether that includes nights. CareScout’s 2025 Cost of Care Survey puts the Illinois median for a non-medical caregiver at home at $36 an hour; its monthly figure of $6,864 is based on 44 hours of care a week. The same survey’s Illinois median for assisted living is $6,219 a month for a private, one-bedroom unit. Original Medicare does not fill the gap at home: among the things Medicare.gov says Medicare doesn’t pay for are “24-hour-a-day care at your home” and personal care when that is the only care your parent needs, and its long-term care page says Medicare doesn’t pay for long-term care; a parent in a Medicare Advantage Plan or another Medicare health plan should check with the plan about its home health benefits. For a parent aged 60 or older who lives in Illinois, is a U.S. citizen or an eligible non-citizen, has non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less, is assessed as at risk of nursing facility placement and applies for Medicaid, Illinois’s Community Care Program can pay for help at home; for a parent aged 65 or older who qualifies for Medicaid and would otherwise need nursing facility care, the Supportive Living Program is the state’s assisted-living-style alternative to a nursing home.

What the Illinois figures say

CareScout’s Cost of Care Survey 2025 collected prices from July through November 2025 and publishes a median for each state and type of care. These are the Illinois figures, as the survey’s own tables state them:

  • A non-medical caregiver at home (in-home care): $36 an hour. The survey’s monthly figure, $6,864, is not a price anyone quoted; it is based on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, divided by 12.
  • Adult day health care: $91 a day. The monthly figure, $1,977, is based on 5 days a week for 52 weeks.
  • Assisted living: $6,219 a month, the monthly rate as reported for a private, one-bedroom unit.

Read the two monthly figures with their bases. The in-home figure buys 44 hours of a caregiver’s time a week; the assisted living figure is the community’s monthly rate. Neither tells you what your parent would pay, and both are statewide medians, not Evanston prices. The guide to what assisted living costs in Illinois explains what the assisted living median measures and the charges that appear after a move.

When you put the options side by side, list the costs of keeping your parent’s home running (housing, utilities, food) next to the in-home figure, because the survey’s in-home rate is a price for a caregiver’s hours. Then ask each assisted living community for its written fee schedule, so you can see what its monthly rate covers.

Comparing what each option costs is a different question from knowing how much help your parent needs, and when.

What Medicare pays for at home, and what it does not

Medicare.gov says Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) covers certain home health services for a person who needs part-time or intermittent skilled services and is “homebound”. Covered services include part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care; physical therapy, occupational therapy and speech-language pathology services if your parent meets certain conditions; and part-time or intermittent home health aide care only if your parent is also getting skilled nursing care or therapy at the same time. In most cases, the page says, part-time or intermittent means skilled nursing and home health aide services “up to 8 hours a day (combined), for a maximum of 28 hours a week.” It adds that your parent may be able to get more frequent care for a short time (less than 8 hours a day and up to 35 hours a week) if the provider decides it is necessary. For covered home health services under Original Medicare, Medicare.gov says: “You pay nothing for covered home health services.” For a parent in a Medicare Advantage Plan or another Medicare health plan, the page says to check with the plan about its home health benefits. The page also notes that a home health agency in Illinois may be part of a Medicare demonstration in which the agency may ask Medicare for a pre-claim review of coverage, which helps the family and the agency know earlier whether Medicare is likely to cover the services.

The same page lists what Original Medicare doesn’t pay for, including “24-hour-a-day care at your home”, homemaker services unrelated to your parent’s care plan, and “Custodial or personal care that helps you with daily living activities (like bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom), when this is the only care you need”.

Medicare.gov’s long-term care page is headed “Medicare doesn’t pay for long-term care”, and says non-medical long-term care services can be had “at home, in the community, in an assisted living facility, or in a nursing home.” So the everyday help that makes up most of either option, at home or in assisted living, is not something Medicare pays for. The long-term care page says this of Medicare and most health insurance, including Medicare Supplement Insurance (Medigap). It adds that a person may be eligible for long-term care through Medicaid, if they meet their state’s eligibility requirements, or can choose to buy private long-term care insurance. The guide to Medicare home health care covers who qualifies and how it starts.

The home health page also says: “You can still get home health care if you attend adult day care.”

What Illinois pays for at home: the Community Care Program

The Illinois Department on Aging’s Community Care Program “helps older adults who might otherwise need nursing home care to remain in their homes by providing in-home and community-based services.” Its services include care coordination, adult day services, in-home services, emergency home response services and an automated medication dispenser service.

The Department lists who is eligible: people 60 or older; U.S. citizens or eligible non-citizens within specific categories; residents of Illinois; with non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less (the home, car and personal furnishings are exempt); with an assessed need for long-term care, measured by the Determination of Need (DON) assessment; and who apply for and, if eligible, enroll in Medicaid.

On cost, the Department’s in-home care page says: “Persons who meet CCP eligibility criteria and enroll in the program receive services at no out-of-pocket cost.” It describes the program’s In-Home Service as delivered by home care aides on a daily or weekly basis. Services are arranged through local Care Coordination Units, which the Department’s map tool finds; the Department’s Senior HelpLine is 1-800-252-8966 (711 TRS). A family member (including a spouse) or a friend can be paid as the caregiver only after meeting the program’s qualifications, passing a background check, completing 24 hours of pre-service training and being hired and supervised by an In-Home Services agency. The guide to the Community Care Program covers the assessment and how to apply.

If your parent pays for help at home

The Illinois Department of Public Health licenses Home Services Agencies to employ home care aides for help with daily living and personal care, and the Department on Aging says: “Clients served by Home Services Agencies pay out-of-pocket for care.” IDPH’s licensing questions and answers list the payers a Home Services license can accept as private pay (out of pocket) or private insurance, with Medicaid clients served through the Community Care Program or the Department of Human Services’ Division of Rehabilitation Services.

Skilled care at home is licensed separately: IDPH licenses Home Nursing Agencies and Home Health Agencies, and the Department on Aging says that, depending on the circumstance, Medicare, Medicaid and/or private insurance may cover those services. If you hire a caregiver directly instead of through an agency, the Department says to check the IDPH Health Care Worker Registry to ensure the caregiver is eligible to work in home care; the guide to checking a home caregiver explains how.

What Illinois pays for in an assisted living setting: Supportive Living

Illinois runs the Supportive Living Program, which the Department of Healthcare and Family Services describes as an alternative to nursing home care for low-income older persons and persons with physical disabilities under Medicaid. Its resident fact sheet calls it an “Affordable assisted living model” for people 65 or older, or 22 to 64 with physical disabilities, who would otherwise need nursing facility care.

Residents choose from a menu of services that includes personal care, medication oversight, meals and snacks, housekeeping, laundry, intermittent nursing services and 24-hour response and security. Medicaid pays for the services; HFS says: “The resident is responsible for paying the cost of room and board at the facility.” The fact sheet adds that most residents have both a room and board charge and a monthly share of the cost of care, worked out from their income. Certified Supportive Living providers are exempt from the Assisted Living and Shared Housing Act, the law under which IDPH licenses assisted living establishments. HFS also says each Medicaid-eligible resident must have income at least equal to the current SSI amount and must contribute all but $120 each month to the provider for lodging and meals, and that certified providers can charge a different rate to residents who pay privately. The guide to the Supportive Living Program covers eligibility and the monthly charges.

The questions that decide it

  • How many hours of help a day, and on which days? The survey’s in-home monthly figure assumes 44 hours a week. Count the hours your parent actually needs before comparing it with a monthly rate.
  • Does your parent need someone at night? Medicare.gov says Original Medicare doesn’t pay for 24-hour-a-day care at home, so overnight help at home is paid for some other way; a parent in a Medicare Advantage Plan or another Medicare health plan should ask the plan what its home health benefits include.
  • Is the need mainly during the day? The Community Care Program describes adult day service as being for older adults who cannot be home alone during the day, and as respite for family caregivers, especially those who are employed outside the home.
  • Could your parent qualify for the Community Care Program or for Medicaid? The asset limit, the assessment and the Medicaid application above decide which public programs are open.
