Life Plan contracts and entrance fees in Illinois, explained

Short answer

A Life Plan community usually charges an entrance fee plus a monthly fee, but what the entrance fee buys depends on the contract. Some contracts include future care with little change to the monthly fee; others give access to care at market or discounted rates. In Illinois, a contract that pairs an entrance fee with care for life, or for more than a year, is a “life care contract”: the community needs a state permit, must give you a statement of its finances before you pay, and must give you a 14-day window to cancel for a full refund. After that, a refund, or a date by which it must be paid, exists only if your contract promises it, and even a promised refund can be reduced or lost if the community fails.

Is this community covered by Illinois’ life care law?

Not every independent-living community is. The Illinois Life Care Facilities Act covers a contract that does all three of these things:

  • requires an entrance fee, paid up front or promised for later;
  • provides food, shelter and laundry, plus personal care, nursing or medical services;
  • runs for life, or for more than one year.

A community offering that kind of contract needs a life care permit from the Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH). Entering into one without a permit is a crime, a Class A misdemeanor.

A rental community with no entrance fee, or a lease that promises no care, usually falls outside the law. Assisted living and nursing homes are licensed separately by IDPH under their own laws, on a Life Plan campus or off one. “Life Plan community” and “CCRC” are industry names; the law looks at the contract.

How to check. IDPH says about 90 campuses in Illinois hold life care permits, most of them in the Chicago area. To confirm that a community holds one today, email IDPH’s Life Care Program at DPH.Lifecare@Illinois.gov with the community’s name and address. A permit puts a community under IDPH’s financial oversight, but it is not a rating: the law requires every permit to say it is not an approval, recommendation or endorsement.

A contract tells you what happens if care is needed. It cannot tell you whether care is needed now.

What the two fees pay for

The entrance fee secures what the contract offers. The monthly fee pays for the housing, services and any care the contract includes. Exactly what each covers is in the contract and its fee schedule, not in state law.

How much care the entrance fee buys depends on the type of contract. The industry uses letter labels, described in a 2010 federal government review:

  • Type A, “extensive” or “life care”: the monthly fee changes little if you move to assisted living or nursing care.
  • Type B, “modified”: some care is included; after an initial period you pay market or discounted rates for more.
  • Type C, “fee-for-service”: you pay market rates for care as you need it.
  • Type D, “rental”: generally no entrance fee.

These letters are shorthand, not Illinois legal categories, and two contracts with the same letter can differ. Read what your contract says happens to the monthly fee when care is needed.

Will you get the entrance fee back, and when?

That depends entirely on the contract. Find four answers in each refund clause:

  1. How much comes back.
  2. Whether that amount shrinks the longer you live there.
  3. When it is paid, and whether payment waits for a new resident to move in.
  4. Who receives it: you, a spouse, or your estate.

Timing matters as much as the amount, and it is two separate questions.

  • When the wait starts. For the refund figures in the notice below, Illinois law starts counting once your apartment is permanently vacated, ready to resell and your account is paid up, or 30 days after you move out with your account paid up, whichever comes first. Delays caused by estate problems outside the community’s control, such as probate disputes, are left out of those figures.
  • When payment is due. Illinois law sets no deadline for paying a refund after you move out or die. A contract may promise a date, or it may not. In a 2010 federal review, community officials said refunds usually waited until a new resident moved into the vacated home, so a slow market meant a longer wait.

What Illinois requires, since 1 January 2025. If an entrance fee is refundable, the community must give you a paper notice before you sign or pay anything. It is titled “ENTRY FEE REFUNDS: CONSUMER NOTICE,” and you sign it before the contract. For the last full calendar year, it shows:

  • the average and the median number of months refunds took;
  • of contracts still waiting for a refund at year-end, the share waiting more than 24, 36 and 60 months;
  • how many contracts are still waiting, and how many refunds were paid.

The notice must also warn that past refund times may not predict yours, and that your “ability to collect on the full amount of the calculated refund may be modified or nullified pending market conditions, any future sale of this organization, or in the event of bankruptcy.” Current residents, former residents waiting for a refund, and their estates can ask for the latest figures.

Sometimes a community wants to use an empty apartment for something else, such as storage, while former residents wait for refunds. Whoever is owed those refunds, which may be an estate, must first agree in writing, up to a set date, on a notice titled “ENTRY FEE REFUND DELAYS: CONSUMER NOTICE.” The rights it describes cannot be signed away.

If the community gets into financial trouble. The 2010 federal review also found that refund claims were generally unsecured in a bankruptcy, so residents could lose the refundable part of their fee. State officials told it they believed such losses were rare; it described one, in Pennsylvania in 2009.

What Illinois law protects, and what it leaves to you

The law requires:

  • A life care permit from IDPH.
  • A statement of the community’s finances, including short-term assets and liabilities, at or before signing and before you pay anything.
  • A 14-day right to cancel with a full refund. The 14 days start on the first full day after the last of three events: signing, your first payment, or receiving the financial statement. Nobody can be required to move in before they end.
  • For refundable contracts, the refund-timing notice above.
  • For a home no one has lived in yet, usually in a new building, entrance fee payments held in escrow at an Illinois financial institution, or covered by a letter of credit. Escrowed money is released in stages as sales and construction milestones are met.
  • A reserve, in escrow or as a letter of credit, covering at least the next six months of payments on the community’s mortgage or other long-term debt.
  • An audited financial statement filed with IDPH every year.
  • If IDPH’s Director believes a community may be unable to keep its contracts, a court case filed through the Attorney General on behalf of residents.

The law leaves to your contract:

  • How much of the entrance fee is refunded, and by when.
  • What happens when care needs change.
  • What happens if you can no longer pay.
  • How much the monthly fee can rise.

And it does not judge the community or guarantee refunds. The permit is not an endorsement. Beyond money still held in escrow, the law guarantees no refund if a community fails.

In a 2010 review of eight states, federal auditors found that Illinois required comparatively few disclosures. Illinois has since added the refund-timing notice. For everything else you have to ask, and the checklist below covers it.

When care needs change

This is the promise you are paying for, so get it in writing. On a Life Plan campus, assisted living, memory care and nursing care may sit next to the independent-living apartments. The contract, not the life care law, decides:

  • whether you have priority for a place in those settings, or join a waitlist;
  • what happens if no place is free: care elsewhere, and who pays for it;
  • who decides that you need to move, and what say you, your family and your doctor have;
  • what your monthly fee becomes after a move;
  • what happens to your apartment, and to a spouse who stays in it.

Those care settings are licensed by IDPH under their own laws, and their inspection records are public. How to check an Illinois care community’s inspection record shows where to look.

If the money runs out

Illinois’ life care law does not answer this, so the contract has to. Ask before you sign:

  • Does the contract promise any help if you can no longer pay the monthly fee? Is there a resident assistance fund, and is it a written promise or a policy that can change?
  • If Medicaid might pay for nursing care later, does the campus’s nursing care accept Medicaid?

Federal Medicaid law can also count a refundable entrance fee as a resource when someone applies. It counts to the extent the fee could pay for care once other income and savings run short, if a refund would be due on leaving or death, and if the fee gives no ownership stake. An elder-law attorney can tell you how that applies to a specific contract.

Ten questions to ask before you tour

Ask them to send these first:

  1. Do you hold a life care permit from IDPH? Then confirm it with IDPH.
  2. Your financial disclosure statement and your latest audited financial statements.
  3. A sample contract and the residents’ handbook. If the two disagree, which one controls?
  4. If any plan is refundable, your “ENTRY FEE REFUNDS: CONSUMER NOTICE” with the latest figures.
  5. The fee schedule, and how the monthly fee changed in each of the last five years.

Ask on the visit:

  1. Exactly how much of the entrance fee comes back, to whom, and by what date? If the contract sets no date, what does payment wait for?
  2. If care is needed, is a place promised or waitlisted, who decides, and what does the monthly fee become?
  3. What happens if we can no longer pay, and does your nursing care accept Medicaid?
  4. Can we talk with two residents about what surprised them after they moved in?

Before signing:

  1. Have an attorney who practices elder law in Illinois read the contract, and a financial adviser if you use one. Keep a copy of everything you sign. The 14-day cancellation clock starts after the last of signing, paying, or receiving the financial statement.
