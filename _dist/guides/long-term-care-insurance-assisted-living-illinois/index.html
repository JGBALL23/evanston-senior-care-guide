My parent has long-term care insurance. Will it pay for assisted living?

Short answer

It can, if the policy covers assisted living, your parent meets its benefit triggers and the elimination period has run. The Illinois Department of Insurance lists “care in an assisted living facility” among long-term care services, and states that “Long-term care insurance will pay for some or all of your long-term care.” Whether it pays for your parent depends on the words of their own policy, so the first step is to find it and read three parts: who qualifies, where care is covered and when payments start.

Start with the policy, not the sales brochure

The Department of Insurance tells policyholders to read the policy and make certain they understand it, and adds: “If you have questions about your insurance policy, contact your insurance agent for clarification. If you still have questions, turn to your state insurance department or insurance counseling program.” It also states that “Medicare, Medicare supplement insurance and health insurance you may have at work usually will not pay for long-term care,” so a long-term care policy is a separate document from your parent’s Medicare card or supplement.

The rest of this guide draws on the National Association of Insurance Commissioners’ Shopper’s Guide to Long-Term Care Insurance (revised 2019), which the Illinois Department on Aging posts on its SHIP pages. It describes how policies are commonly written, not what your parent’s policy says. Where the two differ, the policy decides.

Whether a policy will pay is a separate question from which level of care your parent actually needs now.

Does the policy cover assisted living at all?

Policies differ on where care is covered. The Shopper’s Guide warns: “If you’re not in the right type of facility (described in your policy), the insurance company can refuse to pay for your care.” Some policies pay in any state-licensed facility, some only in certain ones, and some list facilities they exclude. It adds that “many states, companies and policies define assisted living facilities differently,” and that if a policy lists kinds of facilities you should check whether it requires the facility to have a license or certification from a government agency.

Before a move, ask the insurer in writing whether the community you are considering meets the policy’s definition, and ask the community for its Illinois licence details to send with the question. The guide advises checking with the insurance company before making plans for care.

Does your parent meet the benefit triggers?

“Benefit triggers” are how the insurer decides your parent is eligible. The guide says to look for the section the policy calls “Eligibility for the Payment of Benefits” or “Eligibility for Benefits.” It describes three kinds:

  • Help with daily activities. The most common test, the guide says, is that “you are expected to be unable to do two ADLs without human assistance for 90 days. Most policies use six ADLs: bathing; continence; dressing; eating; toileting; and transferring.” Some policies require hands-on help with the activity; others count stand-by help from someone nearby. Which one your parent’s policy uses matters when you describe their needs.
  • Cognitive impairment. “Coverage of cognitive impairment is especially important if you develop Alzheimer’s disease or other dementia.”
  • A doctor’s certification. Some policies require a doctor to order or certify that care is medically necessary; the guide notes that tax-qualified policies cannot use this trigger.

Keep copies of the doctor’s notes, any assessment a community has done and a plain record of the help your parent needs each day: they are the evidence you can offer against whichever test the policy uses.

When payments start: the elimination period

Many policies do not pay from the first day. The Shopper’s Guide states that the elimination period “can be 20, 30, 60, 90 or 100 days before benefits begin,” and that “you must pay for your own care during the elimination period before benefits can begin. Companies don’t pay for care provided by family members during or after the elimination period.”

How the days are counted matters. Under a calendar-day method, every day your parent meets the triggers counts, though the guide notes that “many coverages will not start counting those days until you incur costs.” Under the other method, “only the days that you pay for professional care services covered by the policy count toward the elimination period.” Ask the insurer which method applies, whether days already spent with paid help at home count, and, in the guide’s words, “if the insurance company requires another elimination period for a second stay.”

Budget for the elimination period as a private-pay stretch. The assisted living cost guide gives the published monthly figures for Illinois.

Two policy features to check on day one

  • Waiver of premium. “Premium waiver lets you stop paying the premium once you’re eligible and the insurance company starts to pay benefits.” The guide adds: “Some companies waive the premium as soon as they make the first benefit payment. Others wait until you’ve received benefits for 60 to 90 days.” Keep paying until the insurer confirms in writing that the premium is waived.
  • Third-party notice. A policyholder can name someone the insurer must contact before coverage ends for unpaid premiums. The guide explains why: “Without this notice, people with cognitive impairments who forget to pay the premium might lose their coverage when they need it the most.” If your parent has not named you, ask the insurer how to add a contact.

If the claim stalls or is refused

The Department of Insurance’s complaint page sets out the order. First: “Contact the insurance company or agent and bring the problem to their attention. Document your phone calls by noting the name of the person you speak to, the date of the call and a brief summary of the conversation. Keep copies of all written communications.”

If that does not resolve it, the Department handles complaints that “may include coverage issues, claim disputes, premiums, policy cancellations, refunds, and sales misrepresentations.” Its Consumer Assistance line is (866) 445-5364, but complaints must be made in writing: online through the IDOI Help Center, by email to DOI.complaints@illinois.gov, or by post. The page states that “Illinois law allows 21 days for an insurer or agent to respond to a complaint.”

Know its limits before you write. Among the things the page says the Department cannot do are “Make medical judgments” and “Act as your lawyer or give you legal advice.” A dispute about whether the insurer followed the policy is its business; a dispute about how impaired your parent is may need the doctor’s evidence, or a lawyer.

Where the policy fits in the bigger picture

A policy pays only up to the limits written in it, so it may not settle the whole question of paying for care. If savings may run out later, Medicaid does not simply pick up an assisted living bill. One Illinois Medicaid route into this kind of residence is the Supportive Living Program, which HFS describes as “an alternative to nursing home care for low-income older persons and persons with physical disabilities under Medicaid”; there, HFS says, “The resident is responsible for paying the cost of room and board at the facility.” The Supportive Living Program guide explains it, and the Illinois Medicaid long-term care guide covers nursing home care and care at home. The Illinois Department on Aging’s SHIP page on long-term care insurance lists its Senior HelpLine, 1-800-252-8966.
