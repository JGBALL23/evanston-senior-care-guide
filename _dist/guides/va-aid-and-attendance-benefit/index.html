Does the VA pay for senior care? Aid and Attendance, explained

Short answer

Yes, for veterans and some surviving spouses who already qualify for a VA pension. Aid and Attendance and Housebound are not stand-alone benefits — they are increased monthly rates added to the Veterans Pension, or to a surviving spouse’s Survivors Pension, for someone who needs help with daily activities, is bedridden, is a nursing home patient because of a disability, or has severely limited eyesight. The published figure is a maximum annual rate, not a flat cheque: VA pays the difference between that ceiling and the person’s own countable income, so this guide will not calculate what any one family would actually receive.

What Aid and Attendance and Housebound actually are

Many Evanston families have a veteran parent and no idea this exists, because VA.gov describes Aid and Attendance and Housebound as something you can only get if you get a VA pension — they sit on top of a pension, rather than standing on their own. Mixing the two up with the base pension is the most common way a real benefit gets missed.

VA pays the Aid and Attendance rate to someone who meets at least one of these, quoted directly from VA.gov:

  • “You need another person to help you perform daily activities, like bathing, feeding, and dressing”
  • “You have to stay in bed—or spend a large portion of the day in bed—because of illness”
  • “You’re a patient in a nursing home due to the loss of mental or physical abilities related to a disability”
  • “Your eyesight is limited (even with glasses or contact lenses you have only 5/200 or less in both eyes; or concentric contraction of the visual field to 5 degrees or less)”

The Housebound rate is separate: it is for someone who, in VA.gov’s words, will “spend most of your time in your home because of a permanent disability (a disability that doesn’t go away).” VA.gov is explicit that a person cannot receive both at the same time — it is one increased rate or the other, never both stacked together.

Qualifying for a VA pension tells you that your parent can get help paying for care. It does not tell you which level of care their health actually calls for.

Who qualifies for the pension underneath it

Before either add-on can apply, the veteran has to qualify for the Veterans Pension itself. Two things must both be true. The first is the discharge, and the bar is lower than most families assume: VA.gov requires only that the veteran “didn’t receive a dishonorable discharge” — not that the discharge was honourable. An other-than-honorable or bad-conduct discharge may still make a claim ineligible, and VA.gov describes two routes in that case: asking for a discharge upgrade, or requesting a Character of Discharge review. Do not rule a parent out because the paperwork does not say “honorable.” The second is income and net worth within limits Congress sets.

The service record then has to clear one of three bars, and which one depends on when active duty began:

  • Started before 8 September 1980: at least 90 days of active duty, with at least one day during a wartime period.
  • Enlisted, started after 7 September 1980: at least 24 months, or the full period ordered, with at least one wartime day.
  • An officer who started after 16 October 1981: had not previously served at least 24 months on active duty.

The wartime periods VA recognises include:

  • World War II — 7 December 1941 to 31 December 1946
  • Korean conflict — 27 June 1950 to 31 January 1955
  • Vietnam era — 1 November 1955 to 7 May 1975 for service in the Republic of Vietnam; 5 August 1964 to 7 May 1975 for service elsewhere
  • Gulf War — 2 August 1990 through a future date to be set by law or presidential proclamation

Separately, the veteran must meet at least one of: aged 65 or older; a permanent and total disability; a long-term nursing home patient because of a disability; or already receiving Social Security Disability Insurance or Supplemental Security Income.

The published rate is a ceiling, not a cheque

VA.gov’s rate tables (current as of the cost-of-living increase effective 1 December 2025) give the Maximum Annual Pension Rate, or MAPR — the most a veteran with no dependents can be paid, before their own income is subtracted:

  • Pension alone: $17,441 a year with no dependents, $22,839 with one
  • Housebound: $21,313 a year with no dependents, $26,710 with one
  • Aid and Attendance: $29,093 a year with no dependents, $34,488 with one

That is a ceiling, not an entitlement. VA.gov is explicit that pension pays the gap between the MAPR and the veteran’s own countable annual income, which it defines as including wages, Social Security benefits, investment income and a dependent’s income. Unreimbursed medical expenses can reduce that countable income, but only the portion above 5% of the MAPR counts as deductible — VA.gov’s own words are that these expenses “may reduce your income for VA purposes.” This guide will not do that subtraction for a hypothetical family; the real figure depends on income and medical expenses only VA can verify.

Worth sizing honestly before you plan around it. The highest Aid and Attendance rate for a veteran with no dependents, $29,093 a year, works out to roughly $2,424 a month — against a median Illinois assisted living cost of $6,219 a month, from this site’s cost guide. At the ceiling it covers a useful share of the bill, not the bill. Aid and Attendance is a contribution toward care costs, and it is not a programme that pays for assisted living.

Net worth is capped separately, at $163,699 for the period running 1 December 2025 through 30 November 2026, and it includes a spouse’s net worth too. A house, a car and most home furnishings are excluded.

One figure worth knowing even though it is not the MAPR: a veteran with no dependents who is already a patient in a Medicaid-covered nursing facility has their pension reduced to $90 a month. That is a real VA.gov figure, but it describes a different situation from the Aid and Attendance rate above — do not read the two as the same benefit.

Surviving spouses: the Survivors Pension and its own Aid and Attendance

A surviving spouse who has not remarried, and who generally lived with the veteran from marriage until death (VA.gov allows exceptions for separations caused by health, work, or the veteran’s misconduct), can claim a Survivors Pension if the veteran’s service met the same wartime rules and income and net worth again fall within the limits Congress sets — the same $163,699 net worth limit applies.

Its MAPR figures, for the same period, are lower than a veteran’s own pension:

  • Basic Survivors Pension: $11,699 a year with no dependents, $15,311 with one child
  • Housebound: $14,298 with no dependents, $17,902 with one child
  • Aid and Attendance: $18,697 with no dependents, $22,304 with one child

As with the veteran’s own pension, these are ceilings reduced by the survivor’s countable income, not flat payments.

How to apply

VA.gov lists five ways to apply for the underlying pension:

  • Apply online.
  • Fill out VA Form 21P-527EZ and upload it through the QuickSubmit tool.
  • Fill out VA Form 21P-527EZ and post it to: Department of Veterans Affairs, Pension Intake Center, PO Box 5365, Janesville, WI 53547-5365.
  • Bring the completed form to a VA regional office in person.
  • Get help from an accredited attorney, claims agent, or Veterans Service Organization representative.

Aid and Attendance or Housebound specifically also needs VA Form 21-2680, “Examination for Housebound Status or Permanent Need for Regular Aid and Attendance,” completed in part by a medical examiner. A veteran who already lives in a nursing home must also submit VA Form 21-0779, Request for Nursing Home Information in Connection with Claim for Aid and Attendance.

Applying online while signed in with an identity-verified account sets the claim’s effective date automatically. Applying by mailed form or an unverified account does not get that automatic date, so VA.gov suggests filing an intent to file first to protect it. Either way, be ready with a Social Security number, military and marital history, household income and asset figures, and unreimbursed medical expenses.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

A VA pension answers a different question from Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care? Medicare is federal health insurance and never pays for custodial assisted living; a VA pension is a needs-based cash benefit that a family can put toward any level of care, including assisted living. The two can apply to the same parent at the same time.

This guide has not verified how a VA pension interacts with eligibility for Illinois Medicaid’s long-term care programme — that depends on income counting rules this guide did not read, so it will not guess at the answer. Ask Illinois HFS directly if your parent may need both.
