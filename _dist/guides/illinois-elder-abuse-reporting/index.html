What to do about suspected elder abuse or neglect at home in Illinois

Short answer

Call the Illinois Adult Protective Services (APS) hotline at 1-866-800-1409, 24 hours a day (711 for Illinois Relay). The Illinois Department on Aging says APS “investigates reports of abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community.” That last phrase matters: a problem with the care inside a nursing home or assisted living facility goes to the Long-Term Care Ombudsman or the Illinois Department of Public Health instead. The exception, in the Department on Aging’s words: if a facility resident is abused outside the facility, or financially exploited, by a family member, caregiver or another person with a relationship to them, “the APS Program may respond.”

Two different hotlines, and this guide is about one of them

Illinois splits elder-safety reporting the same way it splits facility complaints: by where the person lives. If your parent lives in their own home, an apartment, or with family — anywhere outside a licensed care facility — the Illinois Department on Aging's Adult Protective Services program is the state channel (and, as the answer above says, it may also respond for a facility resident harmed by a family member, caregiver or someone else with a relationship to them; the Department on Aging’s brochure adds that “The resident may choose to work with the APS Program and/or the Ombudsman Program in this situation.”). Its own programme page states it “investigates reports of abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community.”

A concern about the care a nursing home or assisted living community gives is a different problem with a different number — the Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program or the Illinois Department of Public Health's Central Complaint Registry, both covered in How to report a problem at an Illinois care facility. That guide notes an unexplained number — 1-866-800-1409 — sitting on the Ombudsman Program's page without any description of what it reaches. Having now read the Department on Aging's own APS page, that number is the Adult Protective Services hotline described here, not a facility line.

Reporting a suspected problem is a different question from deciding whether the living situation that let it happen can still be made safe.

What counts as abuse, neglect, exploitation and self-neglect

The Department on Aging's APS Definitions page gives short, plain definitions for each term it investigates. Abuse “means causing any physical, mental, or sexual injury to an eligible adult,” and the department folds financial harm into the same word: abuse includes “exploitation of such adult's financial resources.” Neglect is defined as “another individual's failure to provide an eligible adult with or willful withholding from an eligible adult” the basic necessities of life, “including, but not limited to, food, clothing, shelter, or health care.” Financial exploitation is “use of an eligible adult's resources by another to the disadvantage of that adult” or for “the profit or advantage of a person other than that adult.”

The same page lists self-neglect among the allegations it covers, but the page this guide read does not itself spell out a definition for that term. A family worried that a parent living alone is no longer managing — not eating, not taking medication, not keeping the home safe — does not need a suspect to call; self-neglect on its own is a reportable concern. The Engage page also lists the older adult’s own rights, among them to “Choose where and how they will live” and to “Choose whether to accept social services or other community assistance”, and to make “bad” decisions “as long as those decisions don’t hurt themselves or others”. The same list says that when an older adult “is incapable of protecting himself or herself, under the law that person has the right to have protective measures taken on his or her behalf”.

How to report, and what the state says happens next

The hotline is 1-866-800-1409, which the department’s APS page calls “the statewide, 24-hour Adult Protective Services Hotline”; for deaf and speech-impaired callers, the same page says to dial Illinois Relay at 711. The department's Engage page, aimed at explaining the process to someone thinking about calling, says “Anonymous reports are accepted” and “Your name as the reporter is confidential” — it adds that your identity is not disclosed without your written permission or a court order.

That same page describes what happens after a report reaches a local agency: “a trained caseworker responds within a specified time period depending on the severity of the case,” then talks with the older adult about the risks and what services in the community could lower them — naming caregiver support, housing help and training as examples. The programme page adds that APS is “locally coordinated through 36 provider agencies,” with caseworkers “trained and certified by the Illinois Department on Aging.”

What this guide will not state as a fixed rule

The department's own pages do not give a specific response time — only that it varies with how serious the caseworker judges the situation to be, so this guide will not invent a number of hours or days. On who must report, the Engage page says Illinois law requires certain professionals to report suspected abuse of adults age 60 or older or people with disabilities age 18-59 who are unable, due to dysfunction, to report for themselves: people delivering professional services to them in social services, adult care, law enforcement, education, medicine, state service to seniors and social work. The Department’s Report Abuse page adds that these “Mandatory reporting requirements only apply when the reporter believes that the adult is not capable of reporting the abuse, neglect, or financial exploitation themselves”. A family member does not have to be sure: the same page says “Report it, even if you are not sure.” And while the Engage page mentions, in a single success story, that APS worked with a State’s Attorney in one case, nothing on the pages read states a general rule for when or whether law enforcement gets involved — that single anecdote is not a stated procedure, and this guide will not present it as one.

If a resident's life or safety is at risk right now, call 911 first. Reporting to APS afterward is still worth doing, but it is not built to be an emergency response line.
