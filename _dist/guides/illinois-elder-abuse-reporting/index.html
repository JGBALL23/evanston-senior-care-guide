What to do about suspected elder abuse or neglect at home in Illinois

Short answer

Call the Illinois Adult Protective Services (APS) hotline at 1-866-800-1409, 24 hours a day (711 for Illinois Relay). The Illinois Department on Aging says APS “investigates reports of abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community.” That last phrase matters: APS is for someone living at home or in the community, not in a nursing home or assisted living facility — a suspected problem there goes to the Long-Term Care Ombudsman or the Illinois Department of Public Health instead.

Two different hotlines, and this guide is about one of them

Illinois splits elder-safety reporting the same way it splits facility complaints: by where the person lives. If your parent lives in their own home, an apartment, or with family — anywhere outside a licensed care facility — the Illinois Department on Aging's Adult Protective Services program is the state channel. Its own programme page states it “investigates reports of abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community.”

A concern about a nursing home or assisted living community is a different problem with a different number — the Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program or the Illinois Department of Public Health's Central Complaint Registry, both covered in How to report a problem at an Illinois care facility. That guide notes an unexplained number — 1-866-800-1409 — sitting on the Ombudsman Program's page without any description of what it reaches. Having now read the Department on Aging's own APS page, that number is the Adult Protective Services hotline described here, not a facility line.

Reporting a suspected problem is a different question from deciding whether the living situation that let it happen can still be made safe.

What counts as abuse, neglect, exploitation and self-neglect

The Department on Aging's APS Definitions page gives short, plain definitions for each term it investigates. Abuse “means causing any physical, mental, or sexual injury to an eligible adult,” and the department folds financial harm into the same word: abuse includes “exploitation of such adult's financial resources.” Neglect is defined as “another individual's failure to provide an eligible adult with or willful withholding from an eligible adult” the basic necessities of life, “including, but not limited to, food, clothing, shelter, or health care.” Financial exploitation is “use of an eligible adult's resources by another to the disadvantage of that adult” or for “the profit or advantage of a person other than that adult.”

The same page lists self-neglect as its own allegation type — a report that an older adult's own inability to care for themselves, with no one else at fault, has put them at risk — but the page this guide read does not itself spell out a definition for that term. A family worried that a parent living alone is no longer managing — not eating, not taking medication, not keeping the home safe — does not need a suspect to call; self-neglect on its own is a reportable concern.

How to report, and what the state says happens next

The hotline is 1-866-800-1409, which the department's APS Definitions page gives as the number “to report abuse, neglect, or exploitation,” alongside 711 for Illinois Relay for deaf and speech-impaired callers. The department's Engage page, aimed at explaining the process to someone thinking about calling, says a report can be made anonymously and that “your name as the reporter is confidential” — it adds that your identity is not disclosed without your written permission or a court order.

That same page describes what happens after a report reaches a local agency: “a trained caseworker responds within a specified time period depending on the severity of the case,” then talks with the older adult about the risks and what services in the community could lower them — naming caregiver support, housing help and training as examples. The programme page adds that APS is “locally coordinated through 36 provider agencies,” with caseworkers “trained and certified by the Illinois Department on Aging.”

What this guide will not state as a fixed rule

The department's own pages do not give a specific response time — only that it varies with how serious the caseworker judges the situation to be, so this guide will not invent a number of hours or days. Nor do the pages this guide read state who, by law, is required to report — a mandated reporter page exists and is linked from the department's site, but this guide did not fetch it, so it will not describe mandated-reporter rules on the strength of a link alone. And while the Engage page mentions, in a single success story, that a case was referred to a State's Attorney, nothing on the pages read states a general rule for when or whether law enforcement gets involved — that single anecdote is not a stated procedure, and this guide will not present it as one.

If a resident's life or safety is at risk right now, call 911 first. Reporting to APS afterward is still worth doing, but it is not built to be an emergency response line.
