Does your parent qualify for Extra Help with Medicare drug costs?

Short answer

Extra Help is a Medicare program that pays some or all of a Part D prescription drug plan's premium, deductible and copays for someone with limited income and resources. Medicare.gov states that “some people qualify for Extra Help automatically, and other people have to apply” — automatic qualification comes with full Medicaid, a Medicare Savings Program, or Supplemental Security Income (SSI). Anyone else is compared against an income and resource limit the page restates every year.

What Extra Help actually pays for

Medicare.gov describes “Extra Help” as “a Medicare program to help people with limited income and resources pay Medicare drug coverage (Part D)” costs, specifically premiums, deductibles, coinsurance and other costs. It is not a separate drug plan — it reduces what a parent already owes on whatever Part D plan they are enrolled in, and someone receiving it does not pay the Part D late enrollment penalty that otherwise applies to people who delay signing up.

Paying less for a parent's prescriptions says nothing about whether assisted living, home care or a nursing home is the level of care they actually need.

Who qualifies automatically, and who has to apply

The page states plainly: “Some people qualify for Extra Help automatically, and other people have to apply.” Automatic qualification comes from any one of three things: full-benefit Medicaid, state help paying a Part B premium through a Medicare Savings Program (QMB, SLMB or QI — see Can Illinois help pay my parent's Medicare premium?), or Supplemental Security Income (SSI). If none of those apply, Medicare.gov is direct about the alternative: “In most cases, to qualify for Extra Help, you must have income and resources below a certain limit.”

Medicare.gov's own page on Medicare Savings Programs confirms the link from the other direction: under each of QMB, SLMB and QI it states, “You'll also get Extra Help paying for your prescription drugs,” framed as something that comes with qualifying for the premium programme rather than a second application to fill out.

The 2026 income and resource limits

For someone applying on income and resources rather than qualifying automatically, Medicare.gov's 2026 figures are: for one person, an income limit of $23,940 and a resource limit of $18,090; for a married couple, an income limit of $32,460 and a resource limit of $36,100. The page notes that different limits apply in Alaska and Hawaii without stating the figures, and links out to its own definitions of what counts as income and what counts as a resource — this guide did not open those linked pages, so treat the categories, not just the totals, as worth checking directly before assuming a parent is over or under the line.

What it actually costs once you qualify, for 2027

Medicare.gov lists these as the costs under Extra Help for 2027: a plan premium of $0, a plan deductible of $0, and at a plan's participating pharmacies, up to $5.80 for each generic drug and up to $14.40 for each brand-name drug. Once total drug costs — including certain payments made on a person's behalf, such as through Extra Help itself — reach $2,400, the page states a parent will “pay $0 for each covered drug” for the rest of the year. Someone who also has full Medicaid coverage and is enrolled in the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program pays even less: the page gives that figure as no more than $4.90 for each covered drug.

These are the figures as published for 2027; the income and resource limits above are the ones the same page currently states for 2026. Confirm both against the live page before relying on either, since Medicare republishes both sets of numbers on its own schedule.

How to apply, and what happens once you do

Medicare.gov is specific about the route: “If you don't get Extra Help automatically, you can apply for it” through Social Security, and the page's own application link points to ssa.gov. This guide did not independently open the Social Security site to verify what the application screens look like, so treat ssa.gov/medicare/part-d-extra-help as a name and address to go to, not a page this guide can describe first-hand. Illinois's own Senior Health Insurance Program counselors can also help fill out the application at no charge.

Applying for Extra Help and a Medicare Savings Program can happen at the same time: the page states that Social Security “will send information to your state to initiate an MSP application” unless the applicant says not to on the Extra Help form itself. Once approved, Extra Help runs through 31 December regardless of a mid-year change in income, and someone who still meets the limits keeps it automatically into the next year — a notice only arrives if a parent no longer qualifies or is being moved to a different plan. Anyone can reapply at any time if income or resources change.

If a pharmacy charges the wrong amount, or there is a gap before coverage starts

Medicare.gov's advice if a pharmacy charges more than the Extra Help amount is to contact the drug plan directly, which may ask for proof of Extra Help, and to tell the plan how many days of medication are left on hand; 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227, TTY 1-877-486-2048) is the number the page gives if the plan does not resolve it.

For the gap between qualifying and actually being enrolled in a Part D plan, the page describes Medicare's Limited Income Newly Eligible Transition (LI NET) Program as giving “temporary Medicare Part D drug coverage to people who qualify for Extra Help or Medicaid but who aren't enrolled in a Medicare drug plan yet,” with money back available for covered drugs bought after the qualifying date if receipts are kept. The number given for LI NET is 1-800-783-1307 (TTY 711), 8 a.m. to 7 p.m. Eastern.
