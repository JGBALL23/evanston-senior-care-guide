Will giving money away affect my parent’s Illinois Medicaid eligibility?

Short answer

It can. Illinois HFS reviews the 60 months (five years) before your parent applies for nursing home, Supportive Living, or Department on Aging in-home waiver services, and a gift or a sale for less than something was worth during that window can trigger a penalty period — a stretch of time Medicaid will not pay toward long-term care. Not every transfer counts: HFS says a transfer to a spouse, or a small gift consistent with your parent’s past giving, will not trigger one.

The 60-month look-back, and what it looks back at

Illinois HFS's own brochure for long-term care applicants, “Nursing Home Services & Information for Couples” (HFS 3191), states that transfers made “within 60 months (5 years) of the date you apply for medical coverage” can affect eligibility for three kinds of Medicaid-paid long-term care: a nursing home, a Supportive Living Facility, and the Department on Aging’s in-home waiver services. The brochure also states that this window used to be shorter — 36 months — until the 60-month rule took effect on 1 January 2012.

The look-back is not a separate application your parent has to make. It is something a caseworker checks as part of the same eligibility process covered in How Illinois Medicaid pays for long-term care, and what spend-down means — so a transfer made years before anyone was thinking about a nursing home can still surface the first time your parent applies.

Whether a past gift still counts against your parent today is a different question from whether assisted living, memory care or skilled nursing is the right fit for them now.

What counts as a penalised transfer

HFS states plainly: transferring resources for less than their value “may lead to a period of time Illinois will not help pay for your care,” for the same three services as above. The brochure illustrates this with an example: Joe gave $80,000 to his grandchildren for college within the 60-month window, and “may have a penalty period based on the transfers.” The point of the example is not the dollar figure — it is that a gift made with good intentions, and no thought of Medicaid at all, is treated the same as any other transfer for less than fair value.

Transfers that do not trigger a penalty

HFS carves out specific exceptions, rather than leaving the question to judgment call:

  • Small, consistent gifts. The brochure's own example: Martha's $15 birthday gift was not penalised because it was “a minimal amount consistent with her past behavior.” A pattern of modest giving kept up over years reads differently from a gift that appears only once, near an application.
  • Transfers to a spouse. HFS states your parent “may transfer property to your spouse, or to someone else for your spouse's sole benefit,” and that a spouse may also keep the home, a car, and household furnishings.
  • Returning the property. HFS states a penalty can be eliminated by getting the transferred property back.

This guide is not going to put a current dollar ceiling on the spousal exception — see below for why.

When the penalty starts, and how long it lasts

HFS sets the start date as “the month of application or the month you transferred the resources, whichever is later.” A transfer made long before anyone applies does not start a clock running early; the penalty only begins once your parent has actually applied.

The length is tied to what the transfer would have bought: HFS states the penalty lasts while “the uncompensated value of the transferred property would meet the monthly cost of nursing care at the private rate.” If a second, separate transfer is found and penalised while an earlier penalty is still running, HFS states the new penalty “starts the month after the previous one ends” rather than running at the same time. HFS also states your parent “remain[s] eligible for other medical services” during a penalty — it is specifically long-term-care payment that stops, not all of Medicaid. Your local Family Community Resource Center (FCRC) — the same office handling the application — is who tells you the actual length in a given case; this guide deliberately does not attempt that calculation.

A hardship option if the penalty would leave your parent without care

HFS states your parent can apply for a hardship exception if a penalty would leave them without “food, shelter, clothing or necessary medical care.” The brochure does not set out the hardship application process in the same page this guide read, so ask the FCRC handling the application how to request one rather than assuming the process works the same way as the separate hardship waiver that exists for estate recovery after death — that is a different request, to a different part of HFS.

Figures this guide will not publish

The HFS 3191 brochure itself illustrates the spousal transfer exception and a related income-diversion rule with dollar figures — but the copy this guide read dates those figures to 2015, and the brochure says the spousal limit “may increase yearly.” Eleven years on, this guide has no way to confirm those 2015 numbers still apply, so it will not repeat them. For the amount a spouse at home can currently keep, see the Community Spouse Resource Allowance and Community Spouse Maintenance Needs Allowance figures in How Illinois Medicaid pays for long-term care, which this library verified against HFS's current 2026 notice — that is a related but legally separate rule from the transfer exception on this page, so ask the FCRC which one applies to a specific transfer.
