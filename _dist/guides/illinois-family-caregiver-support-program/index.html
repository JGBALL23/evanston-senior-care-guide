Is there help for the caregiver, not just the parent, in Illinois?

Short answer

Yes — Illinois funds a Family Caregiver Support Program aimed at the caregiver rather than the older adult. The Illinois Department on Aging describes a family caregiver as “an adult family member or another individual who is an informal provider of in-home and community care” to an older person, and says the programme can connect that caregiver to counselling, support groups, training, respite care and limited supplemental services. It is delivered locally through a network of Caregiver Resource Centers and the 13 Area Agencies on Aging, not a single statewide office — so the way in is the Senior HelpLine, not a form.

What the programme actually is

The Illinois Department on Aging says this programme grew out of 2000 amendments to the federal Older Americans Act, which created a National Family Caregiver Support Program; Illinois “was given $4.7 million to develop” its own version with the state’s Area Agencies on Aging and local community-service providers. That is a historical development figure, not a per-family benefit amount, and this guide found no current statewide budget figure published anywhere on the Department’s site.

The Department’s own definition of who counts as a “family caregiver” is broader than a spouse or adult child: it names “an adult family member or another individual who is an informal provider of in-home and community care” to an older person, and separately covers grandparents raising grandchildren. “Informal caregiving,” in the Department’s words, means unpaid care and support given by family or friends to someone with a chronic illness or disability.

What it can connect you to

The Department lists five things the programme does for a caregiver: provides information about available services; helps a caregiver get access to those services; connects a caregiver to individual counselling, support groups or caregiver training; connects a caregiver to respite care, so that caregivers can be “temporarily relieved from their caregiving responsibilities”; and provides supplemental services “on a limited basis” to complement the care a family already gives.

A separate Department page on its Family and Caregiver Resource Centers adds detail to that list: each centre can help locate adult day service, home-delivered meals, housekeeping assistance, home modification and assistive device help, transportation, financial and legal information, caregiver training, case management assistance and employment programmes — on top of the counselling and respite already named.

Respite, in the Department's own words

Respite is the service most caregivers are actually looking for, and the Department's Family and Caregiver Services page describes it plainly: “Respite services are intended to provide caregivers with short-term relief.” It says the length and setting vary — respite “may be provided for an afternoon, several days, at home, or in a facility-based setting.”

The same page says Area Agencies on Aging assess a caregiver's own needs using an assessment tool it calls TCARE, and may refer a caregiver on from there to named programmes such as Savvy Caregiver or the Stress-Busting Program for Family Caregivers, as well as support groups, counselling and what it calls “gap-filling services.”

What this guide could not pin down

None of the three Department pages this guide read states an income limit for the Family Caregiver Support Program, a cost to the caregiver, a cap on respite hours, or a formal application form. The Resource Centers page lists services and regional contacts and nothing about eligibility or fees at all. The Department’s own answer to “Where can I find out about services nearest to me?” is to “inquire at your local Caregiver Resource Center or your local Area Agency on Aging,” or to call the Senior HelpLine.

This guide will not guess at any of those figures on the Department's behalf. If a cost, an income test or a waiting list applies in your case, that answer lives with your local Area Agency on Aging or Caregiver Resource Center, not on a page written in Springfield.

How to start

Call the Senior HelpLine on 1-800-252-8966 (711 TRS for deaf and speech-impaired callers). The Department also publishes a provider search tool for finding the Caregiver Resource Center or Area Agency on Aging that covers your parent's address, since services are organised locally rather than from one statewide office.

If your parent lives outside Illinois, the Department's own guidance is to contact the Caregiver Resource Center nearest to your parent, not to you — the service follows the older adult's location.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

This programme supports the person doing the caring. It is not the same thing as Illinois paying for help at home for the older adult, which is a separate Medicaid-funded programme covered in Can my parent get paid help at home in Illinois? A family can in principle use both at once; this guide found no Department page describing how the two interact, and will not guess.

If the real question is whether home, with or without extra help, is still the right setting for your parent, the free six-question assessment on this site walks through that separately. It asks for no name, no email and no phone number, and your answers never leave your device.
