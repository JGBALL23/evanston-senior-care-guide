Can Illinois help pay my parent's Medicare premium? QMB, SLMB and QI, explained

Short answer

Illinois runs three Medicare Savings Programs — QMB, SLMB and QI — that use Medicaid to pay part or all of a Medicare beneficiary's premium, and QMB also pays Medicare's deductibles and coinsurance. A caseworker decides which of the three a person qualifies for, against income and asset limits the state republishes every April. Medicare.gov's own advice to anyone unsure: “Even if you don't think you qualify, you should still apply.”

Three programmes that pay Medicare costs through Medicaid

The Illinois Department on Aging's own chart describes the Medicare Savings Program plainly: it “is a State Medicaid program that can help to pay Medicare premiums, and possibly deductibles, and coinsurance for Medicare beneficiaries (elderly or disabled) who qualify.” It is not a separate pension or a new Medicare benefit — it is Medicaid money covering part of what Medicare already charges.

Medicare.gov describes three tiers a state runs: the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) programme, the Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB) programme, and the Qualifying Individual (QI) programme. QMB has the widest coverage and the lowest income limit; SLMB and QI pay for less but allow higher income. Medicare.gov also lists a fourth, narrower programme called Qualified Disabled and Working Individual (QDWI), limited to a specific group of people with disabilities who returned to work and lost premium-free Part A. The Illinois chart this guide relied on for state figures does not cover QDWI, so neither does this guide.

Qualifying for help with a Medicare premium says nothing about what kind of care your parent actually needs, or whether assisted living is even the right next step.

What each programme actually pays for

Illinois's own QMB brochure states that the programme covers “payment of Medicare Part A monthly premiums when applicable,” the Part A deductible and coinsurance, the Part B monthly premium and annual deductible, and the Part B 20 percent coinsurance. Medicare.gov adds a practical consequence: once enrolled in QMB, “your provider isn't allowed to bill you” for the services and items Medicare covers, beyond a small Medicaid copayment in some cases.

SLMB and QI are narrower — both pay the Part B premium only, nothing toward deductibles or coinsurance. The difference between them is mainly procedural: Medicare.gov describes QI as “only available for people who don't qualify for any other Medicaid coverage or benefits,” and says it requires a fresh application every year, approved first-come, first-served, with priority given to people who received it the previous year. SLMB carries no such annual reapplication requirement on the Medicare.gov page.

Illinois's income and asset limits for 2026

The Department on Aging's 2026 chart gives these monthly income and resource limits: QMB, set at 100% of the federal poverty level, is $1,330 a month for one person or $1,803 for a couple, with an asset limit of $9,950 for one person or $14,910 for a couple. SLMB, at 120% FPL, is $1,596 for one person or $2,164 for a couple, same asset limits. QI, at 135% FPL, is $1,796 for one person or $2,435 for a couple, same asset limits again.

The chart adds three notes worth repeating rather than smoothing over: the figures are “estimates based on the Federal Poverty Level percentages,” with the exact figures posted by the Illinois Department of Human Services each “Early April”; Illinois automatically disregards $25 of income, so $25 should be added to a household's income before comparing it against the limits above; and the asset limits shown do not yet include an additional $1,500 (single) or $3,000 (couple) burial allowance deduction a household may also claim.

A set of figures this guide will not use

Illinois's own QMB brochure (HFS 3352) states a monthly income limit of $1,073 for one person or $1,452 for a couple, and an asset limit of $7,970 or $11,960 with dependents — figures well below the Department on Aging's dated 2026 chart above. The brochure says these limits “change every April” but never states which year its own numbers reflect, which, set against a chart that is both dated and self-described as the current year's estimate, reads as an older page left live rather than a second current figure. This guide uses the dated chart and leaves the undated brochure numbers out. Confirm your parent's exact current limits when you apply, rather than relying on either number once more time has passed.

The extra benefit that comes bundled in: Extra Help

Medicare.gov states that qualifying for QMB, SLMB or QI brings automatic qualification for Extra Help, the federal programme that caps what a beneficiary pays toward Part D prescription drug costs. For 2026, Medicare.gov gives that cap as no more than $12.65 for each covered drug. A household applying for one of the three Medicare Savings Programs is therefore applying for drug-cost help at the same time, without a separate form.

How to apply

Illinois's QMB brochure directs applicants to their nearest Illinois Department of Human Services Family Community Resource Center (FCRC), where a caseworker can help complete the application; bring a Medicare card, or a letter stating Medicare eligibility, and the FCRC will notify you in writing whether you qualify. Questions can go to the DHS Helpline on 1-800-843-6154 (TTY 1-866-324-5553, relay 11), Monday–Friday 8:30 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., excluding state holidays.

The Department on Aging's 2026 chart names a second route: it tells applicants “you can apply at: https://abe.illinois.gov/,” the state's online Application for Benefits Eligibility portal. This guide did not independently open that site to describe what the application screens look like, so treat it as a name and address to go to, not a description this guide can vouch for beyond what the chart itself says.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

A Medicare Savings Program only ever touches a Medicare premium, deductible or coinsurance — it has nothing to do with paying for assisted living, memory care or a nursing home. For that much larger kind of Medicaid help, see How Illinois Medicaid pays for long-term care, and for what Medicare itself will and will not cover, see Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?
