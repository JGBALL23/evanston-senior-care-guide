Will Illinois take my parent’s house after Medicaid pays for care?

Short answer

Not automatically, and never from your own pocket. Illinois HFS can file a claim against a Medicaid recipient's estate after death to recover what it paid for long-term care — but it “will never ask for more money than it paid for services,” it cannot touch the first $25,000 of any estate with a death on or after 1 July 2022, and it does not pursue a claim at all while a spouse, a child under 21, or a disabled child survives. Heirs are never required to pay a claim from their own money.

What estate recovery actually is

Every state's Medicaid program is required to run an estate recovery program, and Illinois HFS describes why plainly: Medicaid is “a State and Federally funded program,” and after a recipient of long-term services and supports — care at home or in a long-term care facility — dies, the state asks their estate to repay what it spent. HFS's Bureau of Collections runs the program together with the Illinois Department of Human Services.

One limit holds regardless of anything else: HFS states it “will never ask for more money than it paid for services.” A claim is always capped at what the state actually spent on that person's care — it is never a penalty, and it is never calculated any other way.

The claim is against the estate, not against any living person. HFS is direct about this: family members “are not required to pay from their own money.” Nobody inherits a Medicaid bill.

The protections that apply automatically

HFS lists several situations in which it will not seek recovery at all, without anyone having to ask:

  • A surviving spouse is alive
  • There is a child under 21
  • There is a child of any age who is blind or permanently and totally disabled under Social Security's rules
  • The estate is worth $25,000 or less
  • Selling the property would cost more than it is worth
  • Recovery would cause the heirs undue hardship

On top of that list, a separate rule protects part of every estate, regardless of who survives. For deaths on or after 1 July 2022, HFS will not collect against the first $25,000 of estate value at all. HFS illustrates this with its own figures: a $40,000 estate exposes only the $15,000 above that threshold to a claim. If a claim is larger than the estate can cover, the unpaid balance simply stays open in case more assets turn up later — nobody is chased for the difference.

Debts come out of the estate before a Medicaid claim does, too: HFS says funeral costs, legal costs and any mortgage on the home are paid first. And HFS does not offer instalments on what remains — its own FAQ states flatly, “HFS does not allow payment plans.”

What counts as the estate — and what does not

HFS defines the estate as property — money, a house, other valuables — that a person leaves to heirs at death, which in practice means the probate estate. Anything that passes to a named beneficiary outside probate is outside the state's reach. HFS gives two examples directly: a life insurance policy that names who receives the payment, and a bank account that is payable on death to someone else. Its FAQ adds more of the same kind: IRAs, pension plans, jointly held accounts with survivorship rights, mutual funds and deferred compensation plans are generally outside the probate estate and outside a claim.

Property anywhere counts, not just in Illinois — HFS says a claim can reach estate property regardless of location. And if the same person received Medicaid in more than one state, more than one state can file a claim; HFS says the probate court decides how the estate is divided between them.

Liens are a separate, narrower tool

A lien is not the same thing as an estate claim. A lien attaches to real property — land and whatever is built on it — while someone is still alive, rather than after death. HFS describes two situations where one can be filed: cash assistance combined with medical assistance produces a lien equal to the cash assistance received, and medical-assistance-only recipients in a long-term care facility can face a lien, equal to all medical assistance paid before and during the stay, but only after 120 consecutive days in the facility. No lien is filed if the home is occupied by a spouse, a child under 21, a disabled child of any age, or in some cases a sibling, and a lien is released if the resident is discharged home.

This tool has also been narrowed by law. HFS states that Public Act 102-1037, effective 2 June 2022, restricts the filing of new liens on real property as a collection method. Liens already filed before that date are still pursued — the change is forward-looking, not retroactive.

Will the home actually have to be sold?

HFS says the state does not take physical possession of a home. But it is equally direct that the property “may have to be sold in order to satisfy the HFS' claim” once a claim is filed and no exemption applies. Heirs who want to keep the property can instead pay the claim from other funds. And as above, if selling would cost more than the property is worth, HFS will not pursue a sale at all.

The hardship waiver, and a deadline this guide could not verify

Heirs can ask HFS to waive a claim on grounds of undue hardship. Its guide gives examples of what that can look like: the property was a family business, farm or ranch that provided the heirs' main income for at least 12 months before death; paying the claim would mean the heirs need government financial help they do not currently need; or paying it would cause them to lose government help they currently receive. The application is available on the HFS site in six languages, and the heir must request the waiver and supply the proof HFS asks for.

This guide went looking for how long heirs have to request that waiver after being notified, and could not find a deadline stated on either HFS page it read. Secondary sources online quote a 60-day window, but that figure was not read off a primary HFS page, so it does not appear here. If a hardship waiver matters to your family, ask HFS directly — the Department of Human Services Helpline is 1-800-843-6154 (TTY 1-866-324-5553), Monday to Friday — or ask an elder law attorney, rather than relying on a deadline repeated secondhand.
