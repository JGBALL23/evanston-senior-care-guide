Does Medicare pay for hospice care at home?

Short answer

Yes, for the hospice benefit itself. Medicare pays $0 for covered hospice care once a hospice doctor and your parent’s own doctor (if they have one) certify a life expectancy of six months or less, and your parent chooses comfort care instead of treatment to cure the illness. You may still pay up to $5 per prescription for pain and symptom drugs, and 5% coinsurance for a short inpatient respite stay. What it does not pay is room and board if your parent lives in a facility, or anything meant to cure the terminal illness.

What the hospice benefit actually pays for

Medicare describes hospice as “end-of-life care for people with illnesses that can’t be cured,” delivered under Part A. It is not a different, better version of regular treatment — it is a different goal. Once your parent elects hospice, the aim shifts from curing the terminal illness to managing pain and symptoms and being comfortable.

For covered hospice care from a Medicare-approved hospice provider, Medicare pays $0. That is the headline figure, and it is real — but “covered hospice care” is a narrower category than most families assume, and the next two sections set out exactly where its edges are.

Hospice rules say what is covered once a doctor certifies six months or less to live. They say nothing about which kind of care your parent needs before that point is reached.

The four things that must all be true

Medicare lists conditions for electing the hospice benefit, and all of them have to hold:

  • Your parent has Medicare Part A
  • A hospice doctor and your parent’s own doctor (if they have one) certify a life expectancy of six months or less if the illness runs its normal course
  • Your parent accepts comfort care — palliative care for the terminal illness — instead of care intended to cure it
  • Your parent signs a Hospice Election Statement, which must be completed before any hospice services begin

Six months is not a deadline on the care itself. If your parent is still alive after six months, hospice continues as long as a hospice doctor or nurse practitioner recertifies eligibility after a face-to-face visit. People do come off hospice — because they improve, or because they or their family decide to go back to curative treatment — and electing hospice does not forfeit that choice.

What stops being covered the moment hospice starts

Electing hospice changes what Medicare will pay for related to the terminal illness. Medicare states plainly that once the hospice benefit starts, it will not cover any of the following for that illness unless the hospice team arranges it:

  • Treatment intended to cure the terminal illness
  • Prescription drugs meant to cure it, rather than to control pain or symptoms
  • Care from a provider that was not arranged by the hospice team
  • Room and board — at home, in a nursing home, or in an inpatient hospice facility
  • Hospital outpatient or inpatient care, or ambulance transport, unless the hospice team arranges it or it is unrelated to the terminal illness

That last condition cuts both ways. A health problem that has nothing to do with the terminal diagnosis is still covered under Medicare’s ordinary rules, with its ordinary deductibles and coinsurance. Hospice replaces coverage for the terminal illness; it does not replace Medicare for everything else. Before any service outside what the hospice team has arranged, ask the hospice team first — Medicare is explicit that it will not pay for it otherwise.

What it costs

Four cost lines, all from Medicare’s own cost pages for the current year:

  • Covered hospice care itself: $0
  • Prescription drugs for pain and symptom control: a copayment of up to $5 per prescription
  • Inpatient respite care — a short stay that gives a family caregiver a break: 5% of the Medicare-approved amount
  • Room and board in a facility: not paid by the hospice benefit at all

There is a ceiling on the respite copayment. Medicare states that for inpatient respite care, your copayment can’t be more than the inpatient deductible amount. The Part A inpatient hospital deductible is $1,736 for each benefit period, so that is the most 5% can add up to — not a figure most respite stays will come near, but a real limit rather than an open bill.

A figure this guide will not publish. Secondary sites describe the respite coinsurance as capped at a specific dollar amount per day. That is a different claim from the limit above, and the Medicare page this guide read states no daily figure, so none appears here. Ask the hospice provider what a respite stay will actually cost before agreeing to one.

Where care happens, and how long it can continue

Hospice is a service, not a building. Medicare says it is usually provided at home or wherever your parent already lives — including an assisted living or nursing home — and that inpatient hospice facilities are available when certain conditions are met. The same idea applies here as with a skilled nursing stay: it is not the address that decides what is covered, it is whether the hospice benefit has been elected and the services were arranged by the hospice team.

Benefit periods run in a fixed pattern: two 90-day periods, then an unlimited number of 60-day periods, with recertification required before each one past the first six months. Your parent can also change which hospice provider they use once per benefit period.
