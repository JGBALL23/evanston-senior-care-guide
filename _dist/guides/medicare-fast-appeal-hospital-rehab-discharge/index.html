My parent is being discharged from the hospital or from rehab too soon. Can we appeal?

Short answer

Yes, if Medicare is paying for the stay, you can ask for a fast appeal — but the deadline is short. Medicare.gov states you “may have the right to ask for a fast appeal if you think your services are ending too soon (or that you’re being discharged too soon)” from a hospital, a skilled nursing facility and three other kinds of provider. An independent reviewer, the Beneficiary and Family Centered Care-Quality Improvement Organization (BFCC-QIO), decides. In a hospital the request is due “no later than the day you’re scheduled to be discharged from the hospital”; in a skilled nursing facility, “no later than noon the day before the termination date listed on the notice.” The notice your parent is given tells you how to ask.

Two settings, two notices

Everything turns on a written notice, and which one your parent should receive depends on where they are.

  • In a hospital: Medicare.gov states that within 2 days of admission and before discharge, a patient should get a notice called “An Important Message from Medicare about Your Rights,” which it says “is sometimes called the Important Message from Medicare or the IM.” If the hospital gave it more than 2 days before the discharge day, the page states the hospital “must give you a copy of your original, signed IM or give you a new one (that you must sign) before you’re discharged.”
  • In a skilled nursing facility (and with home health, hospice or a comprehensive outpatient rehabilitation facility): the notice is called the “Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage,” and Medicare.gov states you should get it “at least 2 days before covered services end.”

For both, Medicare.gov's instruction is the same: “If you don’t get this notice, ask for it.” Medicare's discharge planning checklist adds: “Read the notice carefully because it contains information about your fast appeal rights.” The notice also states the date your parent's covered services will end — the date every deadline below is counted from.

The deadlines, in Medicare's own words

Hospital. Follow the directions on the Important Message from Medicare “no later than the day you’re scheduled to be discharged from the hospital.” Medicare.gov then states: “If you ask for your appeal within this time frame, you can stay in the hospital while you wait to get the BFCC-QIO’s decision. You won’t have to pay for your stay (except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles).” Miss it, and the page warns that different rules and time frames apply and “you might be responsible for the cost of the hospital stay past the original day the hospital tries to discharge you.”

Skilled nursing facility (rehab). Follow the instructions on the Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage “no later than noon the day before the termination date listed on the notice.” If that deadline is missed, Medicare.gov states “you can request a fast reconsideration from your plan, but services will only be covered if there’s a decision issued in your favor.”

If your parent is in a Medicare Advantage Plan and is in the hospital, Medicare.gov adds: “you can ask your plan for an appeal, but different rules apply.” Read the notice your parent was given, and ask the plan which route applies before the deadline passes.

Who to call

The appeal goes to the BFCC-QIO, which Medicare's checklist describes as “an independent reviewer under contract with Medicare.” The notice itself carries the contact details. Medicare.gov states the BFCC-QIO “is administered for Medicare by Commence or Acentra, depending on what state you live in,” and the checklist adds: “You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE to get the phone number for your BFCC-QIO.” This guide does not print a number for Illinois: use the one on your parent's notice, or the one 1-800-MEDICARE gives you.

What happens after you call

Medicare.gov describes the same sequence in both settings, with different timing:

  • The BFCC-QIO notifies the hospital (and your parent's plan, if they have a Medicare health plan) or the facility.
  • The provider gives your parent a detailed written explanation: in a hospital a “Detailed Notice of Discharge,” by noon the day after the hospital is notified; elsewhere a “Detailed Explanation of Non-Coverage,” by the end of the day the provider is notified. Both state why the services are no longer covered and the coverage rule being applied.
  • The BFCC-QIO asks why you believe coverage should continue and looks at the medical records.
  • It decides: in a hospital “Within one day of getting the requested information”; elsewhere “By close of business the day after it gets the information it needs to make a decision.”

Medicare.gov also states: “You can also ask for copies of any of the materials that were sent to the BFCC-QIO.”

What the decision means for the bill

If the reviewer agrees services are ending too soon: in a hospital, for a parent with Original Medicare, Medicare.gov states Medicare “will continue to cover your hospital stay as long as medically necessary (except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles).” In a skilled nursing facility, the page is more guarded: “Depending on your care needs, Medicare may continue to cover” the care.

If the reviewer agrees services should end: in a hospital, provided the deadline was met, Medicare.gov states “you won’t be responsible for paying the hospital charges (except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles) incurred through noon of the day after the BFCC-QIO gives you its decision.” In a skilled nursing facility: “You won’t be responsible for paying for any SNF, HHA, CORF, or hospice services provided before the coverage end date on the ‘Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage.’ If you continue to get services after the coverage end date, you may have to pay for those services.”

Read that before deciding whether your parent stays on while the appeal is decided: in a skilled nursing facility, a lost appeal leaves the days after the end date on the notice potentially your parent's to pay.

A different problem: inpatient changed to observation

Medicare.gov describes a separate case: “You can ask for a fast appeal while you’re still in the hospital if you were admitted to the hospital as an inpatient and the hospital changed your status to ‘outpatient getting observation services.’” It points to a separate appeal process for that. See Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care? for what Medicare covers after a hospital stay, and what it never covers.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

A fast appeal can buy time; it does not answer where your parent will live and get care afterwards. Medicare's discharge checklist suggests asking “where you’ll get care after you’re discharged” and whether you have options “(like home health care)” — see Medicare home health care for that route, and the levels of senior care for the others.
