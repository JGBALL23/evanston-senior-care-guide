Can a family caregiver get paid by the VA to care for a veteran at home?

Short answer

Sometimes, through the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC) — a VA programme that pays a monthly stipend directly to a family member who provides a veteran’s personal care at home. It depends on the veteran’s disability rating and care need, and on who the caregiver is. A family caregiver who does not qualify is not shut out entirely: VA.gov describes a second, wider programme with no stipend but no relationship or disability-rating test either.

Two programmes, and only one of them pays the caregiver

Families researching VA help with senior care usually find Aid and Attendance first — a cash benefit paid to the veteran. This is a different benefit, paid to the person doing the caregiving. VA.gov runs two separate caregiver programmes, and the distinction is the whole point of this guide: the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC) is the one with a stipend, and it has real eligibility gates on both sides — the veteran and the caregiver. The Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS) has none of those gates, and none of the stipend either. VA.gov states plainly that a family caregiver who does not qualify for PCAFC “may still be able to get support and resources through the Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS).”

Qualifying for a VA caregiver stipend is a question about the family’s own finances. It says nothing about whether the care a parent needs has already moved past what one caregiver, paid or not, can safely provide alone.

What the veteran has to qualify as, for PCAFC

VA.gov lists four requirements for the veteran, and the way it is written, every one of them has to hold:

  • “The Veteran has a VA disability rating (individual or combined) of 70% or higher”
  • “The Veteran was discharged from the U.S. military or has a date of medical discharge”
  • “The Veteran needs at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care services”
  • “The Veteran needs to be enrolled in VA healthcare”

VA.gov describes that personal care need as covering “health and well-being”, help with “daily needs like feeding, bathing, and dressing”, and “safety, protection, or instruction” in the veteran’s own environment — a wide definition that reaches well beyond a single disabling injury. Notably, the page does not name a particular injury, illness or era of service as a requirement; the gate is the disability rating and the care need, not what caused either one.

An active-duty service member facing a medical discharge does not have to wait for separation to start the process: VA.gov says that person “must apply for VA health care before or after applying for caregiver assistance” — the two applications can run in either order.

Who can be the paid caregiver

VA.gov’s own requirements for the caregiver are short:

  • “You must be at least 18 years old.”
  • “You’re a spouse, son, daughter, parent, stepfamily member, or extended family member of the Veteran, or”
  • “You live full time with the Veteran, or you’re willing to live full time with the Veteran”

That second bullet is an “or”, in VA.gov’s own layout, not an “and” stacked on top of the family relationship — someone willing to move in and provide the care can qualify as a caregiver even outside the listed family categories. A veteran can also have more than one approved caregiver at once: VA.gov’s benefits list is written for “eligible primary and secondary family caregivers”, and the stipend below is reserved for the one designated primary.

What a primary caregiver actually receives

VA.gov splits the benefit list in two. Every approved caregiver, primary or secondary, can receive:

  • “Caregiver education and training”
  • “Mental health counseling”
  • “Certain travel benefits when traveling with the Veteran to receive care”

A primary family caregiver — VA.gov’s wording is “eligible primary family caregivers may also receive these benefits” — can additionally get:

  • “A monthly stipend (payment).”
  • Access to CHAMPVA health coverage, for a caregiver who lacks other health coverage
  • “Free legal and financial planning assistance related to the needs of the injured Veteran.”
  • “At least 30 days of respite care per year.”
  • “Access to telehealth therapy sessions through” the programme’s virtual psychotherapy service for caregivers

Primary caregivers must also enrol in direct deposit to actually be paid, through VA’s own customer engagement portal.

A figure this guide will not guess at

Families will want to know how large the stipend actually is. This guide read VA.gov’s own PCAFC page looking for a dollar figure or a formula, and found neither — the page states that the stipend exists and how it is paid, not how it is calculated or what a typical amount looks like. Anything quoting a specific dollar figure for this stipend is not coming from the page this guide could find. Ask the caregiver support team handling the application what the assessed amount would actually be.

If PCAFC does not fit: the Program of General Caregiver Support Services

This is the programme for everyone else, and it is far easier to qualify for. VA.gov’s own conditions: “You care for a Veteran who is enrolled in VA health care”, “You provide personal care services to the Veteran”, and “You want to connect with other caregivers, get more support from a professional care team, or grow your skills as a caregiver.” There is no disability-rating threshold, no family-relationship requirement and no stipend.

What it offers instead, in VA.gov’s own list: “Peer support mentoring”, “Skills training”, “Coaching”, “Telephone support”, “Online programs”, and “Referrals to available resources.” To enrol, VA.gov says to contact the caregiver support team or coordinator at the nearest VA facility, or ask the veteran’s VA health care team for a referral; the veteran has to agree to be cared for by that person and be listed with them in their health record.

How to apply for PCAFC

VA.gov describes one joint process for the veteran and the caregiver together: “You and the Veteran will need to apply together and participate in an application process”, filling out “a joint Application for the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (VA Form 10-10CG)”, both signed and dated. VA.gov lists three ways to submit it: online, by mail to the 10-10CG Evidence Intake Center, PO Box 5154, Janesville, WI 53547-5154, or in person — “bring your completed VA Form 10-10CG to the caregiver support team at your nearest VA medical center.” VA.gov adds one instruction worth repeating exactly: “Don’t send medical records along with your application.”

After that, VA.gov sets its own clock: “We’ll assign you as a caregiver no later than 90 days after we get your application”, with a home-care assessment and required training along the way. Once assigned, wellness check-ins happen “at least once every 120 days, with 1 annual visit to the Veteran’s home.” A decision an applicant disagrees with can be appealed: VA.gov states “you have options to appeal or request a review of the decision.”

For either programme, VA.gov lists a national Caregiver Support Line — 855-260-3274 — alongside a Telecommunications Relay Services number for TTY users, 711. This guide could not confirm the line’s hours on a VA.gov page it read, so it leaves them out rather than repeat a number found only on a facility page.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

This is a fourth, distinct VA path alongside the three this site already covers. Aid and Attendance pays the veteran or surviving spouse a pension they can spend on any kind of care. Veteran Directed Care gives the veteran a budget to hire outside help, which can include a family member, but is not itself a caregiver stipend. VA nursing home and respite care is VA providing or paying for care directly. This programme is the one that pays a family caregiver’s own stipend for care they are already giving. This guide has not verified whether a veteran parent can draw on more than one of these at the same time — VA.gov did not state that plainly on any page this guide read, so it will not guess. Ask a VA social worker about your specific situation.

Illinois separately runs its own caregiver support programme, open to any family caregiver regardless of whether the person they care for is a veteran — see Is there help for the caregiver, not just the parent, in Illinois? The two are not the same programme and this guide has not checked whether a household can use both.
