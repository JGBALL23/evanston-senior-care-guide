Can a family caregiver get paid by the VA to care for a veteran at home?

Short answer

Sometimes, through the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC). It pays a monthly stipend to an approved primary family caregiver of a veteran who, among other requirements, has a VA disability rating of 70% or higher, needs at least six months of continuous, in-person personal care, and is enrolled in VA health care. A caregiver who does not qualify can still get support, without a stipend, through VA’s Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS).

Two programmes, and only one of them pays the caregiver

Families researching VA help often find Aid and Attendance first, an increased rate of a VA pension. VA’s caregiver programmes are different. The Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC) is the one with a monthly stipend, and it has requirements for both the veteran and the caregiver. The Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS) offers training and support instead. VA.gov says family caregivers of veterans who are not eligible for PCAFC “may still be able to get support” and resources through PGCSS.

Whether VA will pay a family caregiver says nothing about whether the care a parent needs has moved past what one caregiver can safely provide at home.

What the veteran has to meet, for PCAFC

VA.gov says: “All of these must be true for the Veteran you’re caring for.”

  • “The Veteran has a VA disability rating (individual or combined) of 70% or higher”
  • “The Veteran was discharged from the U.S. military or has a date of medical discharge”
  • “The Veteran needs at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care services”
  • “The Veteran needs to be enrolled in VA healthcare”

VA.gov says “Personal care services are services the Veteran needs from another person,” and lists three kinds: health and well-being; everyday personal needs, like feeding, bathing and dressing; and safety, protection or instruction in their daily living environment.

Active-duty service members undergoing a medical discharge “will need to apply for VA healthcare,” and VA.gov says they can do that before or after submitting their application for caregiver assistance.

Who can be the paid caregiver

VA.gov’s requirement for the caregiver: “You must be at least 18 years old. And at least 1 of these must be true for you:”

  • “You’re a spouse, son, daughter, parent, stepfamily member, or extended family member of the Veteran, or”
  • “You live full time with the Veteran, or you’re willing to live full time with the Veteran” if VA designates you as a family caregiver

Because only one of the two has to be true, someone outside the listed family relationships can qualify by living with the veteran full time, or being willing to. The stipend goes to the caregiver VA designates as the primary family caregiver; a secondary family caregiver gets a shorter list of benefits.

What a primary family caregiver can receive

VA.gov lists these benefits for both primary and secondary family caregivers:

  • Caregiver education and training
  • Mental health counseling
  • “Certain travel benefits when traveling with the Veteran to receive care”

A primary family caregiver may also get:

  • A monthly stipend (payment)
  • Health care through CHAMPVA, the Civilian Health and Medical Program of the Department of Veterans Affairs, for a caregiver who does not already qualify for care or services under another health plan
  • “Free legal and financial planning assistance related to the needs of the injured Veteran.”
  • “At least 30 days of respite care per year.”
  • Telehealth therapy sessions through VA’s virtual psychotherapy programme for caregivers
  • Access to military commissaries, exchanges and recreation retail facilities

VA.gov adds: “You must be enrolled in direct deposit using the VA customer engagement portal to get your monthly stipend.”

How the stipend is set

VA’s PCAFC page says the stipend exists but not how much it is. VA’s Monthly Caregiver Stipend fact sheet, updated 5 November 2024, explains the formula. It starts from the Office of Personnel Management’s General Schedule annual rate for grade 4, step 1, “based on the locality pay area in which the eligible Veteran resides, divided by 12.” At Level One that monthly figure is multiplied by 0.625. At Level Two, which applies if VA determines the veteran meets the definition of “unable to self-sustain in the community,” it is multiplied by 1.00.

The fact sheet says OPM may, but will not necessarily, update the General Schedule pay tables each year, and that an update takes effect on the first of the month after the month OPM announces it. This guide does not calculate an amount for any family. Ask the caregiver support team at your VA facility what the current figure would be.

If PCAFC does not fit: general caregiver support

VA.gov says PGCSS may be right for a caregiver if all of these are true: “You care for a Veteran who is enrolled in VA health care,” “You provide personal care services to the Veteran,” and “You want to connect with other caregivers, get more support from a professional care team, or grow your skills as a caregiver.” It adds: “You don’t need to be a relative or live with the Veteran to be considered a general caregiver.” The page lists no disability-rating requirement and no stipend.

What it offers: peer support mentoring, skills training, coaching, telephone support, online programmes, and referrals to available resources. To start, VA.gov says to contact the caregiver support team or caregiver support coordinator at your nearest VA facility, or to ask the veteran’s VA health care team for a referral. “The Veteran must agree to receive care from you and have you listed as their caregiver in their health care record.”

How to apply for PCAFC

VA.gov says “You and the Veteran will need to apply together,” and both of you sign and date “a joint Application for the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (VA Form 10-10CG).” It can be submitted online, by mail to the 10-10CG Evidence Intake Center, PO Box 5154, Janesville, WI 53547-5154, or in person: “Bring your completed VA Form 10-10CG to the caregiver support team at your nearest VA medical center.” VA.gov also says: “Don’t send medical records along with your application.”

VA.gov sets its own deadline: “We’ll assign you as a caregiver no later than 90 days after we get your application.” For approved caregivers, VA.gov says wellness contacts happen “at least once every 120 days, with 1 annual visit to the Veteran’s home.” If you disagree with a decision, “you have options to appeal or request a review of the decision.”

For questions about either programme, VA’s Caregiver Support Line is 1-855-260-3274 (TTY: 711), Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. Eastern time.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

This site covers four VA paths, and they are not interchangeable. Aid and Attendance is an increased monthly rate of the VA pension for veterans and some surviving spouses who already qualify for a pension. Veteran Directed Care gives a veteran who qualifies a budget to hire their own help at home. VA nursing home and respite care is care VA provides or pays for itself. This guide covers the programme that can pay a family caregiver directly. It has not verified whether a household can use more than one of these at once, so ask a VA social worker about your situation.

Illinois also runs a caregiver support programme that is not limited to veterans’ families: see Is there help for the caregiver, not just the parent, in Illinois?
