My parent is in an Illinois nursing home and Medicaid is still pending. What happens now?

Short answer

Illinois is meant to decide most long-term care Medicaid applications within 45 days, and when the state runs late, HFS can let the nursing home bill Medicaid before the final decision. HFS calls this Provisional Eligibility; its November 2023 notice states that “The Provisional Eligibility process will occur monthly as long as eligibility determinations exceed the 45 day threshold.” Meanwhile, the HFS brochure for nursing home applicants says eligibility “usually begins with the month you apply” and can reach back up to three months before it (HFS says that falls to two months from 1 January 2027), and that your parent “may also be responsible to pay for some of your long term care services.”

How long Illinois has to decide, and how long it really takes

A parent who moves into a nursing home before Medicaid has approved them is often described as Medicaid pending. HFS’s page for long-term care clients says that if you want the state to pay for nursing home care, you must apply for medical benefits through the Department of Human Services (DHS) and obtain a needs screening through the Department on Aging or DHS.

The Illinois Auditor General’s July 2025 audit of long-term care Medicaid decisions sets out the deadlines: under the federal rules and the Illinois Administrative Code, a decision is due within 45 days for applicants who are not applying on the basis of a disability; for applicants who are, the federal limit is 90 days and Illinois sets 60. The audit also records that “The applicant, his or her spouse, an approved representative, or the facility in which the applicant lives may request a 30-day extension to provide verification of current resources or resources transferred during the look-back period,” and that a second 30-day extension may be allowed on request.

In practice, the auditors tested a sample of 50 applications submitted in 2023 and found that 11 of them (22%) were pending past the required number of days. The report states: “For the 50 applications sampled, it took on average 41 days from receipt of application to decision.” Applications sent to the HFS Office of Inspector General for an asset investigation took longer: “The applications that were referred to HFS OIG spent on average 58 days at the OIG.”

Getting the application decided is a different question from knowing whether a nursing home is the right level of care for your parent in the first place.

Provisional Eligibility: what happens when the state is late

HFS first set up Provisional Eligibility in 2018. Its provider notice of 27 November 2023 explains that delays grew again after the COVID-19 emergency ended, and that “HFS has reimplemented the assignment of Provisional Eligibility for LTC applications which meet the criteria requirements.” Citing the Public Aid Code, the notice states that “individuals given Provisional Eligibility are entitled to receive the same services as individuals with regular Medicaid eligibility regardless of fee-for-service or Managed Care Organization (MCO) plan enrollment.”

HFS’s questions-and-answers document on the process makes four points a family should know:

  • Nobody has to ask for it. “Provisional Eligibility is not requested.” HFS reviews applications and nursing home admissions pending more than 45 days each month.
  • Delays the family causes do not count. “State delays in excess of 45 days in processing submission of a complete application do not include delays caused by incomplete applications or applicant requests for extensions in timeframes to submit required verifications.”
  • A spend-down is not ‘pending’. “Cases in resource spenddown status are not considered pending and will not be eligible for Provisional Eligibility.”
  • It is not the final decision. DHS caseworkers continue to process the application, and the document states that “Questions regarding the status of a pending Medicaid application should be sent to the appropriate DHS office.”

What your parent may owe while the application is pending

The HFS brochure for people applying for nursing home coverage (HFS 3191, revised February 2015 and still linked from HFS’s long-term care page) states that if the application is approved, “your eligibility for medical benefits usually begins with the month you apply, as long as you meet all the eligibility requirements,” and that “you may be eligible for medical benefits for up to three months before the month you apply.”

That brochure predates a federal change. HFS’s page on how federal changes affect Illinois Medicaid states: “Effective January 1, 2027, retroactive coverage will be decreased from 3 months to 2 months for all Illinois Medicaid customers.” For an application made in 2027 or later, count on at most two months of coverage before the month you apply, not three.

The same brochure advises: “If possible, do not pay your medical bills until you learn whether or not you are eligible for medical benefits from the state.” It immediately adds that, depending on income and resources, it may be necessary to pay part of those bills, and that “You may also be responsible to pay for some of your long term care services.” Once a case is decided, “Your local FCRC will tell you how much of your own money you will have to pay to the nursing home or SLF each month.” (An FCRC is a DHS Family Community Resource Center.)

None of the HFS pages read for this guide sets out what a nursing home may bill a resident while an application is pending. Ask the facility’s business office to put in writing what it will charge each month until the decision, and what happens to any payment if Medicaid is later approved for those months. If the application is refused or a penalty period is imposed for gifts made in the five-year look-back, Medicaid will not pay for those months; the look-back guide explains how a penalty is worked out, and the long-term care Medicaid guide explains spend-down.

Contracts, guarantees and being asked to leave

The Department on Aging’s residents’ rights brochure states that a facility “must not require anyone else to sign an agreement stating they will pay for your bill unless they are your court appointed guardian.” Read any admission agreement before a family member signs it.

The brochure lists an unpaid bill as one of four reasons a facility can make a resident leave, alongside danger to themselves or others, medical needs the facility cannot meet, and the facility closing. A written notice is required, and it must say why, explain how to appeal and include a self-addressed envelope to the Illinois Department of Public Health. A resident has the right to appeal within 10 days, and the brochure says to call the Department on Aging at 1-800-252-8966 for help with an involuntary discharge notice. HFS’s brochure adds that “If you receive medical benefits, the nursing home cannot make you leave even if it decides to stop taking care of patients who receive medical benefits.” The residents’ rights guide covers the rest of the brochure.

Keeping the application moving

Most of the delay a family can control is paperwork. HFS’s long-term care application FAQ, written for facilities that file online, states: “Yes, the application may be submitted without verifications. DHS caseworkers will attempt to verify income information electronically. If that is not possible, the caseworker will send a request for the information. An application cannot be processed without all necessary verifications.”

  • Find out who filed the application — the family or the nursing home — and the date it was entered, since HFS counts the 45 days for Provisional Eligibility from the date an application is entered online.
  • Answer every caseworker request by its due date, and if bank or transfer records will take longer, ask for the 30-day extension in writing rather than letting the request lapse. Remember that an extension you ask for is not counted as a state delay.
  • Make sure the needs screening has been done; the pre-admission screening guide explains it.
  • Ask the facility whether a pending application has passed 45 days and been picked up for Provisional Eligibility.

Before your parent moves in

If a move is still being planned, it is worth asking each nursing home how it handles a resident whose Medicaid application is pending, and asking for its answer in writing, before choosing. And if you are not yet sure a nursing home is the right level of care, the free six-question assessment on the home page can help you think it through before you apply.
