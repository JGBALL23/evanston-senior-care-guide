The hospital says my parent is ready to leave, but they can't safely go home. What now?

Short answer

Say so to the hospital staff before the discharge date, not on the day. Medicare’s discharge planning checklist says the doctor and staff “will work with you to plan for your discharge” and that a family caregiver is one of the “important members of the planning team.” It tells a caregiver to make sure they can give the help needed and to talk to the staff about getting help before discharge. If the next step is a nursing home, Medicare’s nursing home guide says the hospital’s staff should be able to help find one and help with the transfer. If your parent has Medicare and you think the discharge is too soon, Medicare.gov describes a fast appeal that must be requested no later than the scheduled discharge day.

You are part of the discharge planning team

Medicare’s publication Your discharge planning checklist is written for patients and their caregivers preparing to leave a hospital, nursing home or other care setting. It says: “You and your caregiver (a family member or friend who may be helping you) are important members of the planning team.” Its first instruction is to use the checklist “throughout your stay”, and it names the people to talk to: the doctor and staff “like a discharge planner, social worker, ombudsman, or nurse”.

The checklist also says: “Ask where you’ll get care after you’re discharged. Do you have options (like home health care)? Tell the staff what you prefer.” If home is not a safe option, that is the moment to say so, while there is still time to arrange something else.

Getting through the discharge itself is a different question from knowing which level of care your parent needs once they leave the hospital.

Tell the staff plainly what help is, and is not, there at home

The checklist asks the patient to circle the activities they need help with and to “discuss your concerns with the staff”: bathing, dressing, using the bathroom and climbing stairs; cooking, food shopping, house cleaning and paying bills; driving to doctors’ appointments and picking up prescriptions.

It has a separate section for the family caregiver. It says: “Make sure you can give the person you’re caring for the help they need.” It asks what tasks the caregiver needs help with and whether they need education or training, and it says to “Talk to the staff about getting the help you need before discharge.” If you cannot provide the care your parent will need at home, this checklist is the hospital’s own Medicare material telling you to raise it with the staff before the discharge happens.

For questions about money, the checklist says to talk to a social worker or your health plan “if you have questions about what your insurance will cover and how much you’ll have to pay.”

If the next step is a nursing home or rehab

Medicare’s Your Guide to Choosing a Nursing Home or Other Long-Term Services & Supports (revised October 2019) says: “If you’re in the hospital, ask your social worker about discharge planning as early in your hospital stay as possible. The hospital’s staff should be able to help you find a nursing home that meets your needs and help with your transfer when you’re ready to be discharged.”

The same guide covers the case where the only nursing home with a bed is one you do not want. The hospital discharge planner or doctor “may be able to help you find a more suitable nursing home or arrange for other care, like short-term home health care, until a bed is available at another nursing home you choose.” It adds a warning: “But you may be responsible for paying the bill for any additional days you stay in the hospital.”

To compare the quality of nursing homes, home health agencies and inpatient rehabilitation facilities near you, the checklist points to Medicare’s Care Compare tool at Medicare.gov/care-compare, or 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227; TTY 1-877-486-2048).

Whether Original Medicare will pay for a rehab stay in a nursing home

Medicare.gov’s skilled nursing facility page says Medicare will only cover care in a skilled nursing facility (SNF) if your parent first has a qualifying inpatient hospital stay: a medically necessary “inpatient hospital stay of at least 3 days in a row (starting the day you were admitted as an inpatient, but not including the day you leave the hospital).” Time under observation or in the emergency room before admission does not count, “even if you’re there overnight.” The page also requires that a doctor or other health care provider has decided your parent needs daily skilled care, given in a Medicare-certified SNF, entered within a short time (generally 30 days) of leaving the hospital.

The page names exceptions to the 3-day rule: a doctor in an Accountable Care Organization approved for a “Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver”, and Medicare Advantage plans, which “may also waive the 3-day minimum inpatient hospital stay.” Its advice is: “Always ask your doctor or hospital staff if Medicare will cover your SNF stay.” Without a qualifying stay, it says to ask about care in other settings, like home health care, or whether other programs, like Medicaid or Veterans’ benefits, can cover SNF care. For what Medicare pays and never pays in a nursing home or assisted living, see Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?; for care at home, see Medicare home health care. Before a move into an Illinois nursing home, the state has its own screening step, explained in Illinois nursing home pre-admission screening.

If you think the discharge is too soon

Medicare.gov’s fast appeals page says that in a hospital, within 2 days of admission and before discharge, your parent should get a notice called the Important Message from Medicare, and “If you don’t get this notice, ask for it.” To appeal, follow its directions “no later than the day you’re scheduled to be discharged from the hospital.” The appeal goes to an independent reviewer, the Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization (BFCC-QIO); the checklist says 1-800-MEDICARE can give you its phone number.

Under Original Medicare, the page says that if you ask in time “you can stay in the hospital while you wait to get the BFCC-QIO’s decision. You won’t have to pay for your stay (except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles).” Miss the deadline and different rules apply, and your parent “might be responsible for the cost of the hospital stay past the original day the hospital tries to discharge you.” For a parent in a Medicare Advantage plan, the page says: “you can ask your plan for an appeal, but different rules apply.” An appeal decides whether Medicare keeps covering the hospital stay; it does not by itself arrange the care your parent needs afterwards, so keep the planning conversation going with the staff at the same time.
