Does my parent need to be screened before moving into an Illinois nursing home?

Short answer

Yes. An Illinois HFS provider notice states that “state statutes require that all persons age 18 or older seeking admission to a nursing facility must be screened,” and says this applies regardless of income, assets, or funding source — a private-pay admission is not exempt. Two separate checks are involved: a Determination of Need (DON) assessment of the functional need for nursing-facility-level care, and a PASRR Level I screen for serious mental illness or an intellectual/developmental disability. Both must be completed before admission.

Two different checks, not one

Families researching this usually find the word “screening” used loosely, as if it were one step. The Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) provider notice this guide read describes two separate checks that both apply before a nursing facility admission.

The first is a Determination of Need (DON) assessment — a functional evaluation of whether the person actually needs the level of care a nursing facility provides. The second is a PASRR Level I screen (sometimes called an OBRA Level I screen), which checks for serious mental illness or an intellectual/developmental disability. The notice states that this preliminary screen applies to “all applicants to Medicaid-certified nursing facilities”. Someone flagged at Level I is referred on for a more detailed Level II evaluation.

Knowing that a screening is required is a different question from knowing whether a nursing home is even the setting your parent actually needs.

Who actually does the screening

The notice names several different screeners, depending on where the person is and what is being assessed. Care Coordination Units (CCUs) — contracted by the Illinois Department on Aging and described on the Department's own Community Care Program page as part of its “Aging Network” — conduct most Level I screens and DON assessments, both in hospitals and in the community.

Hospital discharge planning staff can also carry out an OBRA Level I screen directly. In the community, DHS Division of Rehabilitation Services staff perform DON screens, while DHS Mental Health PASRR agents handle referrals from hospital psychiatric units and community mental health centres. Where a Level I screen suggests an intellectual or developmental disability rather than mental illness, an Independent Service Coordination entity contracted by the DHS Division of Developmental Disabilities handles the nursing-facility level-of-care determination instead.

None of this is a single office or a single phone call. Which screener is the right one depends on where your parent is — still at home, or already in hospital awaiting discharge — and that is worth confirming with a hospital social worker or the local CCU rather than guessing from this list.

Private pay does not exempt you from the screening itself

This is the detail that most surprises families who intend to pay privately and assume Medicaid rules do not apply to them. The screening requirement is a state statute covering every nursing facility admission — the HFS notice is explicit that it applies “regardless of income, assets, or funding source”. Paying the full bill out of pocket does not remove the requirement to be screened before moving in.

HFS's own consumer brochure on long-term care frames the same rule from the other direction, for someone applying for Medicaid: “the screening must show that you need nursing home care or the state will not pay for it.” So the screening does two different jobs depending on who is asking — a legal precondition to admission for everyone, and a payment precondition specifically for Medicaid.

One reassurance worth knowing: the brochure also states that a person keeps the right to enter or remain in a nursing home if they can pay for the care, or qualify for state assistance, even if the screening finds they could live safely in the community. A DON result that says “this person could manage at home with support” is not, by itself, a reason a facility can turn your parent away or discharge them — it mainly governs whether Medicaid will pay, and it is also the moment a care coordinator may raise home and community-based alternatives worth hearing out before deciding.

Timing, and what happens if it is skipped

Both screens have to happen before admission to the nursing facility, not afterward as paperwork to catch up on. The HFS notice sets out minimum timelines for the hospital-discharge case: discharge planners must contact the CCU “at least 24 hours in advance of a pending discharge,” and the notice states that, since 1 January 2017, “CCUs must complete the screening within one day of the request.” This guide is reading a 2017 notice to providers, not a page that states today's timeline, so treat those two figures as the rule as written then and confirm the current turnaround with the CCU rather than assuming nothing has changed since.

The consequence of skipping it is stated plainly: the notice says the state “will continue to reject claims for reimbursement of LTC services provided prior to the completion” of the required screenings, and that Medicaid payment authorization is not allowable until pre-admission screening is complete. For a family counting on Medicaid to eventually cover a stay that starts as private pay, an admission that happened without the screening is not a detail to sort out later.

If your parent is already living in the nursing home

The process is different for someone who is already a resident and only now applying for medical benefits. HFS's brochure states that if you already live in a nursing home when you apply for medical benefits, and you entered the facility on or after 1 July 1996, “a special needs screening is completed by Healthcare and Family Services (HFS)” itself, rather than by a CCU before admission. That is a different screening route from everything described above, run by a different state agency, for a different situation.

What this guide could not verify

The state's own screening-results form (HFS 2536, the Interagency Certification of Screening Results) is the document that would state how long a completed screening remains valid before a new one is needed. This guide opened that form and could not read a verifiable figure off it — the document did not render as text this guide could confirm, as opposed to a page it could read directly. Rather than repeat a number picked up secondhand, this guide will not publish a validity period. Ask the local CCU or the admitting facility's admissions office how long your parent's specific screening result is good for before it needs to be redone.

How to start

If your parent is still at home, the Illinois Department on Aging's Community Care Program page describes CCUs as the local point of contact and directs families to its own map tool to find the right one — the same CCU that performs the DON assessment also coordinates in-home services, covered in full in Can my parent get paid help at home in Illinois? If your parent is currently in hospital and a nursing home admission is being discussed as part of discharge planning, HFS's brochure says to ask the hospital nurse to contact the hospital's social service department, which can set the screening in motion directly rather than waiting for a CCU referral from outside.
