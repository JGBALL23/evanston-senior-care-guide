Can my parent get paid help at home in Illinois?

Short answer

Possibly. Illinois runs the Community Care Program, which the Department on Aging describes as helping older adults “who might otherwise need nursing home care to remain in their homes.” It is for people 60 or older with non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less and an assessed need for long-term care. A house, a car and personal furnishings are exempt from that asset figure. You start by calling the Senior HelpLine on 1-800-252-8966.

What the programme actually provides

The Community Care Program was established in 1979 by Public Act 81-202. The Illinois Department on Aging lists five services under it: comprehensive care coordination, in-home service, adult day service, emergency home response service, and automated medication dispenser service.

In-home service is the one most families are picturing. The state describes it as help with household tasks — cleaning, planning and preparing meals, laundry, shopping and errands — and says homecare aides also assist with personal care such as dressing, bathing, grooming and following special diets.

Adult day service is for someone who wants to stay in the community but cannot be home alone during the day. It also gives a family caregiver hours back, which matters if you are the one holding this together from a job.

Emergency home response is a 24-hour link to help outside the home, for people with documented health and safety needs and mobility limitations. Automated medication dispenser service dispenses or prompts non-liquid oral medication and notifies a responsible party about a missed dose.

Who qualifies

The Department on Aging lists these requirements. A person must be 60 years old or older; a resident of Illinois; either a U.S. citizen or an eligible non-citizen within the specific categories; have non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less; have an assessed need for long-term care; and apply for Medicaid and enrol if eligible.

The asset figure is narrower than it sounds. The state states plainly that a home, a car and personal furnishings are classified as exempt assets, so owning the house your parent lives in does not by itself put them over the line.

The need test is run with a state tool called the Determination of Need, or DON, which measures whether someone is at risk of nursing facility placement. This guide does not publish a passing score. The threshold is set by the state and applied by an assessor, and a number copied off a website is not the number that will decide your parent's case. Ask the assessor what the current threshold is.

The part that confuses everyone: two agencies, one programme

You will see this called the Community Care Program in one place and the Persons who are Elderly waiver in another. They are not two programmes. Illinois HFS — the state Medicaid agency — states that the Persons who are Elderly waiver “is a component of the Community Care Program (CCP) provided through the Illinois Department on Aging.” HFS administers and oversees it; the Department on Aging runs it day to day.

HFS also names the target groups inside that waiver: people who are aged, 65 and older, and people who are physically disabled, ages 60 through 64. And it lists two conditions the Aging page does not — that the estimated cost to the state for home care is less than the estimated cost of institutional care, and that the person can be safely maintained at home with the services in the plan of care.

Read that second condition carefully, because it is the honest limit of this programme. It pays for help at home while home is still safe. It is not a promise that home will always be the answer.

How to start

Call the Senior HelpLine on 1-800-252-8966. HFS instructs callers to choose a language and then press 4. Office hours are Monday to Friday, 8:30 to 5:00. For deaf and speech-impaired callers, Illinois Relay is 711.

Assessments are carried out locally by organisations the state calls Care Coordination Units. The Department on Aging publishes a map tool for finding the one that covers your area, and that unit is who actually visits and scores the assessment. If your parent is already in a Medicaid managed care plan, HFS says to contact that plan's care coordinator about in-home services rather than going around them — and if you do call the local unit directly, tell them the plan name and have the identification number ready.

One thing to know before you plan around any of this: HFS lists a proposed amendment to the waiver with an effective date of 1 January 2027. Rules in this programme change. Confirm the current ones on the call rather than trusting a page — including this one.

When home help is not enough

This programme and a move are not rivals, and plenty of families use one then the other. If the concern has shifted from help to safety — wandering, the stove, not recognising the house — homemaker hours are not the tool. Assisted living, memory care, skilled nursing and rehabilitation — what is the difference? sets out what each setting actually provides.

If the money is the obstacle rather than the care level, Illinois also pays for assisted-living-style care in approved buildings through a separate programme, explained in Illinois Supportive Living Program, explained.
