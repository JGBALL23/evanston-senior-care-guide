Does Medicare pay for assisted living or nursing home care?

Short answer

Medicare does not pay for assisted living, and it does not pay for long-term custodial nursing home care. It pays only for short-term skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospital stay: days 1–20 cost $0, days 21–100 cost $217 each day in 2026, and from day 101 you pay everything.

The distinction that decides everything

Medicare divides care into skilled and custodial, and that single distinction decides whether it pays anything at all.

Skilled care is nursing or therapy that, in Medicare's words, can only be safely and effectively performed by, or under the supervision of, professionals or technical personnel — intravenous medication, wound care, physical therapy after a fracture. Custodial care is help with bathing, dressing, eating, getting to the toilet, medication reminders and supervision.

Medicare pays for skilled care, short-term, under conditions listed below. It pays nothing toward custodial care, however much of it someone needs and however long they paid into the system. Assisted living is custodial care. That is the whole reason Medicare does not cover it.

It is not the building that decides this — it is the stay. The same nursing home room can be covered one month and not the next. A qualifying short-term rehabilitation stay in a Medicare-certified facility is covered. If that same resident stays on in that same bed because they can no longer live alone, the stay has become long-term custodial care and Medicare pays none of it. Families who were told "Medicare is covering mom’s nursing home" are almost always describing the first situation and planning for the second.

Knowing what Medicare covers is a different question from knowing what kind of care your parent actually needs. Coverage follows the care; it does not decide it.

The five conditions, all of which must hold

Medicare requires every one of these for a skilled nursing stay to be covered. One missing condition is enough to make the whole stay private-pay:

  • A qualifying inpatient hospital stay — at least 3 days in a row, counting from the day of inpatient admission and not counting the day of discharge
  • Admission to the facility generally within 30 days of leaving the hospital
  • A doctor’s decision that daily skilled care is needed
  • The care given in a Medicare-certified skilled nursing facility
  • Skilled services for a condition treated during that hospital stay, or one that arose while being treated for it

The trap inside the first condition. Hours spent under observation or in the emergency room do not count toward the three days, even if your parent was in a hospital bed overnight. A family can spend four days in a hospital and have zero qualifying days. Ask, out loud, on the first day: has my parent been admitted as an inpatient, or are they under observation? Then ask again, because the status can be changed.

If your parent is in Original Medicare and the hospital changes the status from inpatient to outpatient observation, Medicare.gov says your parent can ask for a fast appeal while still in the hospital; patients currently in the hospital get a Medicare Change of Status Notice (CMS-10868). It also says why the change matters: Medicare will not cover a skilled nursing facility stay within 30 days of leaving the hospital after it. If the notice does not come, Medicare.gov says to ask for it. Medicare.gov also says it is best to appeal while still in the hospital, but your parent can still ask for an appeal after leaving: by following the instructions on the notice or, without the notice, through the local BFCC-QIO, the organization that decides these appeals.

Two exceptions exist. A doctor in an Accountable Care Organization with a Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver may not need the three days, and Medicare Advantage plans may waive it too. Medicare.gov's advice is to always ask your doctor or hospital staff whether Medicare will cover the stay, and, if your parent is in a Medicare Advantage plan, to contact the plan.

If your parent did not have a 3-day qualifying inpatient hospital stay and needs care after leaving the hospital, Medicare.gov says to ask whether care can be given in another setting, such as home health care, or whether another programme, such as Medicaid or veterans’ benefits, can cover the skilled nursing stay.

What a covered skilled nursing stay costs in 2026

These are the costs of a qualifying skilled stay only. Nothing below applies to long-term custodial care, assisted living or memory care, where the Medicare share is zero. On a covered stay, Part A pays on this schedule:

  • Days 1–20: $0 coinsurance each day. The Part A deductible of $1,736 may still apply for the benefit period — “$0 coinsurance” is not “free”
  • Days 21–100: $217 each day
  • Day 101 and beyond: you pay all costs

You do not pay the Part A deductible twice. If it was already paid during the hospital stay in the same benefit period, it is not charged again for the skilled nursing stay.

Run the arithmetic before you rely on the 100 days. A full run of days 21 to 100 is $17,360 out of pocket, and coverage stops entirely at day 101.

If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan rather than Original Medicare, the rules differ — some plans charge copayments during the first 20 days. Ask the plan directly, in writing.

Coverage can end early — but not just because someone stopped improving

Coverage can stop before day 100, and you may be told the reason is that your parent has stopped improving. That is not the standard.

Under the Jimmo v. Sebelius settlement, CMS confirmed there is no “Improvement Standard”. Coverage does not turn on whether someone has potential to get better. It turns on whether they need skilled care. Skilled nursing and skilled therapy are covered where those services are needed to maintain the person’s current condition, or to prevent or slow further deterioration, safely and effectively — even when no improvement is expected at all.

Coverage does properly end when the requirements for skilled care are no longer met — for example, when the care a resident needs can be delivered safely and effectively by non-skilled staff, or when the 100 covered days in the benefit period run out. That is a different test, and it is the correct one.

So the practical move is two sentences long. Ask the discharge planner on day one what length of covered stay is expected, and ask to be told in writing the moment that assessment changes. If the reason given is that your parent is no longer improving, say the word Jimmo, ask for the decision in writing, and ask how to appeal. A written notice is what gives you weeks instead of days.

Choosing or changing a Medicare plan when a move may be coming

Medicare.gov sets the dates. During the Open Enrollment Period, 15 October to 7 December each year, your parent can join, drop or switch Medicare Advantage plans and can “Switch from Original Medicare to a Medicare Advantage Plan or from a Medicare Advantage Plan to Original Medicare.” A change made then starts on “January 1 of the next year (the plan must get your request to join by December 7)”.

From 1 January to 31 March there is a second window, the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period, which Medicare.gov says is “only if you’re already in a Medicare Advantage Plan”: your parent can switch to another Medicare Advantage plan, or “Drop your Medicare Advantage Plan and return to Original Medicare.” A parent who returns to Original Medicare this way can also join a separate Medicare drug plan. A parent in Original Medicare cannot use this window to join a plan.

Some moves open their own window. Medicare.gov lists a Special Enrollment Period for someone who lives in, or recently moved out of, an institution such as a nursing home or rehabilitation hospital: “Your chance to join, switch, or drop coverage lasts as long as you live in the institution and for 2 full months after the month you move out of the institution.” A parent who moves to an address outside their Medicare Advantage plan’s service area, for example to be near family in Evanston, can switch plans too; Medicare.gov says that chance “begins when you move and continues for 2 full months after you move.” If your parent tells the plan before the move, that chance begins the month before the month of the move. A parent who moves out of the plan’s area can also go back to Original Medicare, and one who joins no new Medicare Advantage plan in that time is enrolled in Original Medicare when the old plan drops them. A move to a new address that is still in the plan’s area but brings new plan options opens a window to switch as well.

These address-move windows are for someone already in a plan. The pages cited here list no window tied to the move for a parent in Original Medicare with no plan who moves within the U.S. to somewhere that is not an institution, such as assisted living; for that parent, ask 1-800-MEDICARE or use the Open Enrollment Period. (A parent moving back to the U.S. after living abroad has 2 full months after the month of the move to join a plan.)

A parent with Medicaid or Extra Help has one more option. For them, the Special Enrollment Periods page says: “If you have Medicaid or get Extra Help, you can make changes to your coverage once a calendar month.” The changes it lists are switching to a different Medicare drug plan, or dropping a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage and returning to Original Medicare by joining a stand-alone Medicare drug plan. Another listing of the same window on that page also allows joining a drug plan, and says “The change will take effect on the first day of the next month.” The page adds that this window is not open to someone identified as a “potential at-risk beneficiary” or “at-risk beneficiary” under a Part D drug management program; the plan sends a letter if so. A parent with Medicare and full Medicaid can also join or switch to an integrated Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan (D-SNP), where one is available, once a calendar month. The page adds: “Remember, not all D-SNPs are integrated.” It says to contact the D-SNP plan you are interested in, or 1-800-MEDICARE for a list of integrated D-SNPs. For how that works in Illinois, see the guide to FIDE SNPs.

Why it matters before a move: as the sections above say, the rules for a skilled nursing stay differ in a Medicare Advantage plan (a plan may waive the three-day hospital stay, and some plans charge copayments during the first 20 days), and the plan your parent holds when they need that stay is the one whose rules apply. For questions about enrollment changes, Medicare.gov gives 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227). In Illinois, free one-to-one help comparing plans comes from the state’s counsellors: see Who helps my parent choose a Medicare plan for free in Illinois?.

So who does pay for assisted living?

Three realistic sources. Private funds, including savings, pension income and the proceeds of a house. A long-term care insurance policy, if one was bought years ago. Or Illinois Medicaid, through the Supportive Living Program, which does pay for care in approved settings.
