Should I sign my parent's nursing home admission contract as the responsible party?

Short answer

Not as a personal guarantee of payment — and a Medicare- or Medicaid-certified nursing home is not allowed to make you. Federal regulation 42 CFR 483.15(a)(3), which binds every Medicare- or Medicaid-certified nursing facility, states the facility “must not request or require a third party guarantee of payment to the facility as a condition of admission or expedited admission, or continued stay in the facility.” Medicare's own consumer guide lists, among things a nursing home isn’t allowed to do: “Make a third party (like your family) pay your nursing home bill.” It may only ask a representative with legal access to your parent’s own money to use that money to pay, and the Illinois Department on Aging’s residents’ rights brochure states a similar limit in its own words.

The federal rule, in the regulation's own words

The requirement sits in the Medicare and Medicaid requirements for participation every certified nursing facility must meet. CMS's current surveyor guidance (QSO-25-14-NH) quotes the regulation directly: “The facility must not request or require a third party guarantee of payment to the facility as a condition of admission or expedited admission, or continued stay in the facility.” The same guidance states plainly who that protects: “The prohibition against third-party guarantees applies to all residents and prospective residents in all certified long term care facilities, regardless of payment source.” Your parent's ability to private-pay does not remove this protection.

What is still allowed is narrow and specific. CMS's guidance continues: “the facility may request and require a resident representative with legal access to the resident’s funds available to pay for facility care to access and use the resident’s money or other assets to pay for care, as authorized by law. The facility may request and require this representative to sign a contract, without incurring personal liability, to provide the facility with payment from the resident’s income or assets.” That is a promise to pay from your parent's money if you control it — not a promise to pay from yours.

Language that counts as noncompliant, even without the word “guarantee”

CMS's guidance is explicit that a facility cannot get around the rule by avoiding the word “guarantee.” It states: “language can be noncompliant even if it does not specifically reference a ‘guarantee’ by a third party. Any language contained in an agreement that seeks to hold a third party personally responsible for paying the facility would violate this requirement.” The guidance lists examples of what that covers, including:

  • Language that holds both the resident and a representative or other individual jointly responsible for sums due to the facility (naming the resident alone is allowed).
  • Language that holds a representative personally liable for “breach of an obligation in the agreement,” such as not applying for Medicaid in time.
  • Language that does not mention a guarantee but “implies the resident could be discharged if the representative does not voluntarily agree to personally pay to prevent the discharge.”
  • Language that holds a representative personally liable for unpaid amounts because they did not supply accurate financial information or report a change in the resident’s finances.

CMS's guidance also states this is not limited to the main admission agreement: noncompliant language is a problem “if it appears in the main document that a facility uses as its admission agreement or in other documents that are signed at admission,” and a facility “cannot use such language in agreements regarding a resident’s continued stay” after admission either.

What Medicare tells families directly

Medicare's own consumer guide, Your Guide to Living in a Nursing Home, puts the same rule in plain language under the heading “Read your admission agreement carefully”: “You aren’t required to agree to everything. For example, a nursing home isn’t allowed to: Make a third party (like your family) pay your nursing home bill. It may only ask your family to use your money to pay the bills (if your family has legal access to your funds).” The same guide also flags a separate clause worth watching for: a nursing home “isn’t allowed to” require you to sign a binding-arbitration agreement, resolving disputes outside court, as a condition of admission or care.

Medicare's practical advice if something in the agreement concerns you: “cross out the item with a pen or marker before signing to show you don’t agree.” It also suggests asking a lawyer to review the agreement first, if you have one.

How Illinois's residents' rights brochure puts it

Illinois’s Department on Aging publishes a residents’ rights brochure for long-term care facilities that states the same protection in its own words, with a differently worded exception from the federal rule: “Your facility must not require anyone else to sign an agreement stating they will pay for your bill unless they are your court appointed guardian.” Notice the difference from the federal exception above: Illinois’s brochure names a court appointed guardian, while the federal rule speaks more broadly of a representative with “legal access” to a resident's funds. This guide read both documents as written and is reporting that difference rather than resolving it — which one applies to a specific representative (an agent under a power of attorney, for example, versus a court-appointed guardian) is a question for the facility, the Long-Term Care Ombudsman, or an elder law attorney, not something this guide will guess at.

A gap this guide could not close: assisted living

CMS’s guidance says the federal rule above, 42 CFR 483.15(a)(3), applies to “all certified long term care facilities”; none of the three sources this guide read addresses assisted living or shared housing contracts. This guide searched for an equivalent Illinois government page addressing responsible-party or guarantor language specifically in assisted living contracts and did not find one on an allowed host. If your parent is moving into assisted living rather than a nursing home, do not assume the same federal protection applies — read carefully any signature line that would make you a responsible party or guarantor, and ask the establishment in writing what liability it creates before signing.
