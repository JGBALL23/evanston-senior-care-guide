Does Medicare pay for a wheelchair, walker or hospital bed at home?

Short answer

Yes, with conditions. Medicare Part B covers durable medical equipment — including wheelchairs, scooters, walkers, hospital beds, canes and commode chairs — when a doctor or other health care provider orders it for use in your parent's home. After the Part B deductible ($283 in 2026), you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount, and only if the supplier is enrolled in Medicare and accepts assignment. Power wheelchairs and scooters need a face-to-face exam and a written prescription first, and certain types of power wheelchairs need Medicare's prior approval before Medicare will cover them.

The rule that applies to all of it

Medicare.gov states the same condition on its walker, cane and commode-chair pages: “Part B covers medically necessary DME if your doctor or other health care provider orders it for use in your home.” The wheelchair page adds that Part B covers power wheelchairs and scooters “only when they’re medically necessary” — no order, no coverage, regardless of how obviously your parent needs the item.

Once that order exists, the cost-sharing is also the same across every item: “After you meet the Part B deductible, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount (if your supplier accepts assignment).” The Part B deductible is $283 for 2026. That last clause — “if your supplier accepts assignment” — is not a formality, which is why it gets its own section below.

These are the terms of Original Medicare. If your parent gets Medicare through a Medicare Advantage plan, Medicare’s booklet on equipment coverage says the plan must cover “the same medically necessary categories of DME items as Original Medicare”, but that “the suppliers you get your DME from and your specific costs will depend on which Medicare Advantage Plan you belong to.” It says to contact the plan to find out if it will cover the equipment, and that if the plan will not cover an item you believe your parent needs, you can appeal the plan’s denial and get an independent review.

Knowing Medicare will pay most of the cost of a wheelchair or a hospital bed is a different question from knowing whether your parent's needs have already moved past what a piece of equipment at home can solve.

What is actually covered, item by item

  • Wheelchairs and scooters — Medicare.gov: “Medicare Part B (Medical Insurance) covers wheelchairs and power-operated vehicles (scooters) if you're eligible,” but only “when they're medically necessary”
  • Walkers, including rollators — “Medicare Part B (Medical Insurance) covers walkers, including rollators if you're eligible”
  • Hospital beds — “Medicare Part B (Medical Insurance) may cover hospital beds, if you're eligible”
  • Canes — covered, with one named exception: “Medicare doesn't cover white canes for the blind”
  • Commode chairs — “Medicare Part B (Medical Insurance) covers commode chairs,” on the same doctor’s-order and home-use terms

That cane exclusion is worth sitting with for a moment. Two items that look alike to a family — a mobility cane and a cane for someone who is blind — are covered differently, and Medicare.gov’s cane page states the exclusion without giving a reason for it. The lesson is not about canes specifically; it is that “covered” is decided item by item, on each item's own Medicare.gov page, not by a general sense that medical equipment is covered.

The supplier matters as much as the doctor's order

Medicare.gov is direct about this, repeated near-verbatim across its equipment pages: “Medicare will only pay for DME items if you get them from suppliers who are enrolled in Medicare.” An order from your parent's own doctor does not help if the equipment comes from a supplier Medicare has not enrolled — the claim is not covered, full stop.

Enrollment alone is not the end of it. The walkers page adds: “If a supplier participates in Medicare, they must accept assignment,” and for anything rented rather than bought, “make sure the supplier is willing to accept assignment for all rental months, otherwise you’ll have to pay the full cost of your DME upfront.” In that case, the page says, “Medicare will pay you later for the amount it covers after your claims have been submitted and processed by Medicare.” The hospital-beds page spells out the risk: “If a DME supplier doesn’t participate in Medicare or won’t accept assignment, you may be charged more.” Ask the question before the equipment is ordered, not after the first bill arrives.

Wheelchairs and scooters have an extra step

Power wheelchairs and scooters carry a requirement the other items on this page do not. Medicare.gov's wheelchairs and scooters page states you must have “a face-to-face examination and a written prescription from a treating provider” before Medicare will cover one.

On top of that, the page states you “may have to get prior approval” for “certain types of power wheelchairs,” and says “your DME supplier will usually submit a prior authorization request and all documentation to Medicare on your behalf.” Medicare can still say no — the page describes a denial when “Medicare finds you don’t medically require a power wheelchair” or “Medicare doesn’t get enough information to make a decision.” A family that orders a power wheelchair expecting the same process as a cane or a walker can be caught out by this extra step; ask the supplier, before anything ships, whether prior authorization applies to the specific model being ordered.

Rent or buy is not always your choice

Medicare.gov describes renting and buying as both normal, without settling in advance which applies to which item: “You may need to rent the equipment” and separately “you may need to buy the equipment,” and in some cases “you may be able to choose whether to rent or buy the equipment.” None of the equipment pages this guide read states which rule applies to a wheelchair versus a hospital bed specifically — that is a question for the supplier before you commit, not an assumption to make from this page.

One detail softens a long rental: “Some items become your property after you've made a certain number of rental payments.” If your parent will need an item for years rather than months, ask the supplier directly how many rental payments that conversion takes for the specific equipment being ordered.
