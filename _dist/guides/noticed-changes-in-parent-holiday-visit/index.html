You noticed changes in your parent during a holiday visit. What should you do next?

Short answer

Write down what you saw, book your parent’s doctor, and make one call to the state’s Senior HelpLine. Medicare Part B covers a yearly “Wellness” visit in which the provider will “Perform a cognitive assessment to look for signs of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease.” The Illinois Department on Aging’s Senior HelpLine, 1-800-252-8966, says its staff can “evaluate your needs and answer your questions” and connect you with local care coordination services. If you fear your parent is in danger or neglecting themselves, the 24-hour Adult Protective Services Hotline is 1-866-800-1409.

Start with what you saw, in writing

If you do not see your parent every week, a holiday visit can show changes all at once. Before you call anyone, write down what you noticed and when: the things your parent found hard, what was different from your last visit, and anything that worried you about safety. Keep it factual and short. It gives the doctor and anyone else you speak to something concrete to work from, and it lets brothers and sisters who were not there see the same picture.

Medicare.gov lists the kinds of change a provider looks for in a memory check: “Signs of cognitive impairment include trouble remembering, learning new things, concentrating, managing finances, and making decisions about your everyday life.” Those are useful headings for your notes. A list is not a diagnosis, and only a clinician can say what is behind a change.

Seeing that something has changed is a different question from knowing what kind of help your parent now needs.

Try the free six-question assessment — about two minutes, no name, no email and no phone number, and your answers never leave your device. It is educational guidance, not a diagnosis.

Book the doctor: the yearly Wellness visit and its memory check

Medicare Part B covers a yearly “Wellness” visit once every 12 months. Medicare.gov says of it: “It isn’t a routine physical exam.” During the visit, the provider will “Perform a cognitive assessment to look for signs of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease.” The page adds that if the provider thinks there may be cognitive impairment, “Medicare covers a separate visit to do a more thorough review of your cognitive function and check for conditions like dementia, depression, anxiety, or delirium.”

On cost, the Wellness visit page says: “You pay nothing if your provider accepts assignment.” It also says your parent may have to pay coinsurance, and the Part B deductible may apply, if the provider performs additional tests or services during the same visit that this preventive benefit does not cover. For the separate cognitive assessment visit, Medicare.gov says: “After you meet the Part B deductible, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount.” Both pages describe Medicare Part B; they do not say what a Medicare Advantage plan charges, so a parent in one of those plans should ask the plan.

Medicare.gov also says that a first Wellness visit cannot take place within 12 months of Part B enrollment or the “Welcome to Medicare” preventive visit, and that “If you have specific concerns about your health, you should schedule a separate appointment to discuss those”. If what you saw is a specific worry, say so when you book.

You can go too. The cognitive assessment page says: “When you have an assessment, you can bring someone with you (like a spouse, friend, or caregiver) to help listen to information and answer questions.” Your written notes are most useful there.

Make one call: the Senior HelpLine

The Illinois Department on Aging runs a Senior HelpLine at 1-800-252-8966, Monday through Friday, from 8:30 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.; people who are deaf or have speech impairments can dial 711 (Illinois Relay). The Department says: “We can help you locate services in your community, evaluate your needs and answer your questions, connect you with local care coordination services, and provide helpful information, assistance, and literature.” Its staff write referrals for care coordination services, the Long-Term Care Ombudsman program, legal services, transportation, employment and nutrition services.

The HelpLine page lists what it is helpful to have when you call: your parent’s name, age and address, the services they already receive, and daytime telephone numbers.

Illinois is divided into 13 areas, each served by an Area Agency on Aging. For suburban Cook County, the Department’s list names AgeOptions, Inc., at 1-800-699-9043 (for the suburban Cook County area only) or 708-383-0258. The Department notes that Area Agencies “are not, as a rule, direct service providers”: they contract with local agencies that serve older people in their own communities.

If your parent wants to stay at home

The Department on Aging’s Community Care Program “helps older adults who might otherwise need nursing home care to remain in their homes by providing in-home and community-based services.” Its services include in-home help with household and personal care tasks, adult day services, emergency home response and automated medication dispensers. To be eligible, the Department lists: age 60 or older; U.S. citizen or eligible non-citizen; an Illinois resident; non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less (the home, car and personal furnishings are exempt); an assessed need for long-term care, measured by the Determination of Need assessment; and applying for and, if eligible, enrolling in Medicaid. The Department’s in-home care page says people who meet the criteria and enroll “receive services at no out-of-pocket cost.” The program is arranged through local Care Coordination Units. See the guide to the Community Care Program for more.

Families can also pay for help privately. The same page says the Illinois Department of Public Health licenses Home Services Agencies to employ home care aides for help with daily activities and personal care, and that “Clients served by Home Services Agencies pay out-of-pocket for care.”

If you are worried about safety right now

The Department on Aging’s Adult Protective Services program investigates reports of “abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community.” To report, the statewide, 24-hour Adult Protective Services Hotline is 1-866-800-1409, and the Department lists Illinois Relay at 711 for deaf and speech-impaired callers. The guide to reporting elder abuse in Illinois explains what happens after a report.
