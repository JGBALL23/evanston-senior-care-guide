Independent living in Evanston and the North Shore, explained

Short answer

Independent living is housing with services, not care. A private apartment, cottage or townhouse, with some combination of meals, housekeeping, grounds and transport folded into one monthly fee. Generally no hands-on help with bathing or dressing, and generally no nursing staff on the floor. Nobody has to need anything to move in, which is the point: most people who do it are well, and they do it while they still get to choose.

What you are actually buying

Three things, and it helps to price them separately.

  • The home. An apartment, cottage or townhouse you live in on your own terms.
  • The work you stop doing. Cooking, cleaning, the roof, the gutters, the lawn, the snow, the driving. For most people this is the real purchase.
  • The people. A dining room, a calendar, and neighbours on the other side of the door. Easy to dismiss from a brochure, and the thing residents mention first.

What you are not buying is care. If someone needs help getting dressed, or medications handed to them, or a nurse within reach, independent living is the wrong setting, and a good community will say so rather than take the deposit.

Independent living is the one option on this site that is not care. That makes it the easiest to choose, and the easiest to choose too late.

How it differs from assisted living, in one line

Assisted living is a licensed category in Illinois. Independent living is not.

The Illinois Department of Public Health runs a Division of Assisted Living which, in its own words, “is responsible for all state licensure and survey processes for Assisted Living and Shared Housing establishments”, and has licensed them since regulations were adopted in December 2001. IDPH publishes the list of licensed establishments.

Independent living does not appear on that licence list, and this guide found no state care-licence category for it in Illinois. That is not a loophole and not a warning — it follows from no personal care being provided. It does mean the state inspection records that exist for assisted living and nursing facilities do not exist in the same form here, so the usual way of checking a care community does not apply. Ask any community exactly what it is licensed as, and for which parts of its campus.

What it costs, and the figure this guide will not give you

The CareScout Cost of Care Survey is the source this site uses for Illinois senior-living costs. It reports a statewide median for assisted living of $6,219 a month. It reports no Illinois median for independent living.

So there is no honest statewide number to print, and this guide will not invent one. Anyone quoting a precise Illinois independent-living average is working from something other than that survey.

What moves the price is the shape of the contract. Ask for a written fee schedule that separates four things:

  • Any entrance fee or deposit — and exactly what is refundable, to whom, and when.
  • The monthly fee, and which services sit inside it.
  • What is billed on top: a second meal, a guest room, parking, transport beyond a set radius.
  • How the monthly fee moved in each of the last five years. Ask for the history, not the policy.

Medicare pays none of this. Medicare is health insurance; independent living is housing. The same is true of assisted living, which is the misunderstanding most families arrive with.

Who it tends to suit

There is no eligibility test to pass. Communities set their own minimum age and financial requirements, and some run a health screen to confirm a person does not need more care than the community is licensed to give. Those are the community’s rules, not the state’s.

In practice it suits someone managing fine who has started resenting the building they live in — the maintenance, the stairs, the winter. If the honest answer to “why now” is the house rather than the health, this is the category that answers it.

It also suits planning ahead. Many Evanston and North Shore campuses have assisted living and skilled nursing on the same site, which makes one question matter more than any amenity: what happens if care needs change? Ask whether a resident can stay, what triggers a move, who decides, whether a place in the care building is held for residents or waitlisted, and what the fee becomes. Get the answer in writing.

Visiting, and what a brochure cannot tell you

Go at a mealtime rather than a scheduled tour slot. The dining room answers more than the model apartment does.

  • Ask two residents whether they chose this or were moved here. The answers differ, and the difference is the culture.
  • Check whether the activity calendar on the wall matches what is actually happening that afternoon.
  • Notice whether anyone is eating alone, and whether anyone does anything about it.
  • Ask what last year’s turnover was, and where people went.

Visiting before you need to is the cheapest version of this decision. Nothing obliges anyone to move.
