My parent isn't safe at home but refuses to move. What can we do in Illinois?

Short answer

In Illinois, an adult parent decides where they live unless a court appoints a guardian. The state’s Guardianship and Advocacy Commission states that “The law presumes that an adult eighteen years of age or older is capable of handling his/her own affairs,” and that “In Illinois, the only way someone can be a guardian for a person who is eighteen years old or older is to be appointed by the circuit court.” A guardian of the person can make decisions about residential placement, but the Commission calls guardianship a last resort and says that where possible, alternatives should be pursued first.

Where Illinois law starts

The Commission’s Office of State Guardian is plain that age or illness alone is not enough: “Without more, a mental, physical or developmental disability is not sufficient for the appointment of a guardian.” Guardianship, its fact sheet says, “is needed when a person is unable to make and communicate responsible decisions regarding his personal care or finances due to a mental, physical or developmental disability.”

A parent who makes a choice the family thinks is unwise has not met that test. The Commission’s questions-and-answers page states: “Making incorrect or ill-advised decisions on a periodic basis is not the test. Rather, it is an inability to engage in the decision making in the first place which is important.” It suggests four practical questions:

  • “Does the person understand that a particular decision needs to be made?”
  • “Does the person understand the options available in any decision?”
  • “Does the person understand the consequences of each option?”
  • “Is the person able to properly inform appropriate parties once the decision has been made?”

The same page says that an inability to make sound decisions about matters including where to live and when to seek medical care is an indication that a person may need some guardianship services.

Whether the law lets you decide for your parent is a separate question from what level of care your parent actually needs.

What the state says to try first

The Commission states: “All possible alternatives should be explored before instituting guardianship proceedings.” It names representative or protective payees, financial counselling and bill-paying assistance programmes, living trusts, and homemaker and other in-home support programmes as ways that may avoid the need for guardianship.

It also points to arrangements a parent can make while they still can. Under the Illinois Power of Attorney Act, the page says, a person can appoint an agent to make property or personal and health care decisions, and “The execution of the form requires no court involvement, and forms are readily available.” Under the Health Care Surrogate Act, a relative or friend can make some medical decisions for a person who lacks capacity without a court appointment. The power of attorney guide covers those documents.

If you are worried about self-neglect

When a parent living at home is not managing food, medicine or safety, the Illinois Department on Aging’s Adult Protective Services programme is another route. Its page states that it “investigates reports of abuse, neglect, exploitation, and self-neglect of adults age 60 or older, and adults age 18-59 with disabilities living in the community,” and gives a statewide, 24-hour hotline: 1-866-800-1409. The elder abuse and neglect guide explains what happens after a report.

What guardianship involves

The fact sheet describes two kinds. A “guardian of the person” is appointed when someone cannot make or communicate responsible decisions about personal care, and “This guardian will make decisions about medical treatment, residential placement, social services and other needs.” A “guardian of the estate” handles money and property. Either can be limited, covering only the decisions the court specifies, or plenary, covering all of them.

  • The report. Before going to court, the Commission says, one must obtain a report certifying that the person is disabled and needs a guardian, signed by a licensed physician, based on evaluations performed within 3 months of filing and including a recommendation on the most suitable living arrangement.
  • The petition and hearing. An “interested person” files a petition in the circuit court of the county where your parent lives, and the fact sheet states: “Guardianship hearings are set within 30 days of a petition being filed with the court.”
  • Your parent’s rights. “A person facing a guardianship adjudication has the right to a court appointed attorney and a trial by a jury of six persons.” Most counties also appoint a guardian ad litem to advise the court independently.
  • Emergencies. A temporary guardian can be appointed while the case is pending, and the fact sheet says temporary guardianship “lasts no longer than 60 days” and is used “only where a demonstrated harm or emergency exists.”
  • Time and cost. “Once the case is filed, it usually takes from 14 days to two months for a decision to be reached by the court.” The petitioner usually pays filing fees, sheriff’s fees for serving the summons, and attorney’s fees; the fact sheet adds that “Guardianship can be costly and complicated.”
  • Who serves. “Family members are not automatically named the legal guardian for their disabled relative.” The judge decides. The Office of State Guardian serves only when no other suitable person is willing.

For Cook County, the Commission notes that the rules are in Part 12 of the general rules of practice for the Circuit Court of Cook County, and that the Probate Court Clerk can usually supply the physician’s report form. The Office of State Guardian’s toll-free intake number is 1-866-274-8023.

Before you file

The fact sheet ends with a warning worth reading twice: guardianship “should never be used in a retaliatory manner or as a convenience for a health care provider or a family member.” The Commission also says that once established, a guardianship can be extremely difficult to revoke. Talking to an attorney before filing is sensible; the Commission says an Office of State Guardian attorney or a legal assistance agency can be consulted by a family filing without one.

Often the more useful first step is a calm conversation about what kind of help would actually make home safe, or which kind of setting would. If you are trying to work that out, the free six-question assessment on the home page is a private place to start.
