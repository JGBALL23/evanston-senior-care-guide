Can the VA provide nursing home care, or give a family caregiver a break?

Short answer

Yes, through VA's own long-term care programme — a different path from the Veterans Pension. VA.gov describes three nursing home settings a veteran can use (VA Community Living Centers, VA-contracted Community Nursing Homes, and state-run Veterans Homes), plus a Respite Care benefit that pays for short-term care so a family caregiver can rest. Eligibility turns on enrolment in VA health care, clinical need, and — for nursing home care specifically — service-connected disability status and income. VA.gov is explicit about one limit many families miss: it does not pay room and board for assisted living or an adult family home.

Three places VA provides nursing home care directly

Many families assume VA only ever writes a pension cheque. VA.gov’s long-term care page instead describes three separate nursing home settings a veteran can actually live in, each arranged differently: a VA-run Community Living Center, “designed to feel like home”; a Community Nursing Home — a non-VA facility that VA contracts with; and a State Veterans Home, which is state-owned and managed.

VA’s own page for the first of these is direct about what it is: “A Community Living Center (CLC) is a VA Nursing Home.” It offers nursing-home-level care, including skilled nursing and help with daily activities, and the page says CLCs aim to resemble “home” as much as possible, with family-friendly visiting, room decorating and pets allowed.

Eligibility for any of the three runs through the same gate. VA.gov states that nursing home care is provided “IF you meet certain eligibility criteria involving your service-connected status, level of disability, and income,” on top of being enrolled in the VA health system and “medically and psychiatrically stable.” Clinical need and whether a bed is actually available where your parent lives both factor in too — none of this is a guarantee of a particular setting.

Knowing that the VA will provide or pay for nursing home and respite care answers how your veteran parent gets that care. It does not answer whether a VA pension is still the better way to pay for assisted living instead.

Respite care: a break for the family caregiver, not just the veteran

VA.gov’s newer page describes Respite Care simply as a benefit a veteran “may be able to get” that “gives your family caregiver a break.” Its older Geriatrics and Extended Care page is more specific about who it is for: veterans who need help with daily activities, or whose caregiver is isolated or under strain.

It comes in two forms. Home Respite Care covers “a person to come to a Veteran’s home,” or the veteran attending an adult day health care programme instead. Nursing Home Respite Care is a stay “in a VA Community Living Center or a community nursing home,” which VA.gov says “should be scheduled in advance.”

VA.gov’s eligibility rule: “All enrolled Veterans are eligible for Respite Care IF they meet the clinical criteria for the service and it is available.” Availability varies by location, and if the respite is delivered by a community agency, adult day health centre or nursing home rather than VA itself, the veteran also has to meet separate community care eligibility rules.

This guide could not find a stated annual day limit for Respite Care on either VA.gov page it read, so it will not publish one. Ask your VA social worker or case manager what is actually available at your parent’s facility before assuming a figure you may have seen elsewhere.

What VA will not pay for: assisted living and adult family homes

This is the limit that catches families off guard. VA.gov states it without qualification: “The VA does not pay for room and board in residential settings such as Assisted Living or Adult Family Homes.” Those settings have their own, separate eligibility rules, and a veteran living in one may still be able to receive some of VA’s home and community-based services there.

VA.gov adds that it does not run, staff or pay for these settings, but it does inspect and approve each one it refers a veteran to, and VA benefits may still help pay for extra services received there, such as a visit from a VA provider — a narrower role than VA plays for a Community Living Center, where VA is the licensed operator.

This is exactly the gap Aid and Attendance is built to fill: a cash pension a family can put toward the cost of assisted living that VA itself will not cover directly.

Other home and community services VA names

VA’s older long-term care overview lists “Services at Home and in the Community” available to “all enrolled Veterans” who have a clinical need and local availability: Geriatric Evaluation, Adult Day Health Care, Homemaker and Home Health Aide Care, Respite Care, and Skilled Home Health Care.

Its newer page describes two of these in more detail. Adult Day Health Care is a daytime programme offering “social activities, companionship, and recreation” along with care and support. Home-Based Primary Care sends “a VA healthcare team, led by a VA doctor, who provides services” in the home, such as nursing care and physical therapy.

Neither VA.gov page this guide read describes what Homemaker and Home Health Aide Care or Skilled Home Health Care actually involve beyond their names, so this guide will not guess. Ask a VA social worker which of these are available near your parent.

What it costs

VA.gov’s long-term care overview states one date plainly: “Long term care copays are not charged until the 22nd day of care.” After that, a Community Living Center stay can mean a copay “based on your VA service-connected disability status and financial information,” and a VA social worker or case manager can tell you the amount.

Hospice care is treated differently wherever it is delivered: VA.gov states “Copays are NOT charged for Hospice Care provided in any setting.” For anything VA does not cover, its newer page points families toward Medicaid, Medicare, or their own private insurance.

This guide has not verified how a VA pension interacts with a Community Living Center copay — that depends on income and disability rules only VA can apply to one person’s file, so it will not guess at the result. Ask the social worker handling the Application for Extended Care Benefits.

How to apply

VA.gov’s instruction is the same across Community Living Center care, community nursing home care and Respite Care: contact your VA social worker or case manager and complete VA Form 10-10EC, Application for Extended Care Benefits. That is what sets the copay amount, and the social worker can also help find a facility and make arrangements.

Illinois also runs its own State Veterans Homes

The “State Veterans Home” option on VA’s own list has a local version. The Illinois Department of Public Health states that the Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs runs “all four Illinois Veterans’ Homes,” and that “each Home is licensed by IDPH and certified by the US Department of Veterans Affairs Medical Center of jurisdiction.” IDPH describes the homes as “fully staffed by long-term care professionals and volunteers from veteran service organizations and the local community,” coordinating with the local health care community for each resident’s health needs.

One protection IDPH names specifically: under Public Act 100-0632, if two or more residents in a home are diagnosed with an infectious disease within a month or less, the home must notify residents, their emergency contacts, the Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs and IDPH within 24 hours of the second diagnosis, and post a notice near its main entrance.

This guide could not verify the homes’ locations, admission requirements or cost — the Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs’s own site did not load while researching this guide, and the IDPH page does not restate that detail. Contact the Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs directly before assuming a location or a figure found elsewhere.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

This site now covers three separate VA paths, and they are not interchangeable. Aid and Attendance is a cash pension a veteran or surviving spouse spends on any kind of care, including assisted living. Veteran Directed Care is a budget a veteran controls to hire their own help at home. This guide is the third: VA directly providing or paying for nursing home, respite and other care itself, under its own eligibility rules rather than a pension calculation.

If the open question in your family is which level of care your parent actually needs, rather than which VA programme pays for it, the free six-question assessment on this site walks through that separately.
