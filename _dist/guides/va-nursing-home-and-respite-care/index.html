Can the VA provide nursing home care, or give a family caregiver a break?

Short answer

Yes, for enrolled veterans who meet VA’s criteria — through VA health care, a different path from the Veterans Pension. VA.gov describes three nursing home settings: VA Community Living Centers, community nursing homes that VA contracts with, and State Veterans Homes. It also describes Respite Care, which pays for short-term care so a family caregiver can take a break. For a VA nursing home or a contracted community nursing home, VA ties eligibility to enrollment in VA health care, clinical need and criteria involving service-connected status, level of disability and income. One limit many families miss: VA says it does not pay room and board in assisted living or adult family homes.

Three nursing home settings VA describes

Many families assume VA help with long-term care means a pension cheque. VA.gov’s long-term care page describes three nursing home settings a veteran may be able to use: a VA Community Living Center, one of the “VA nursing centers that are designed to feel like home”; a community nursing home, a non-VA nursing home that VA contracts with so a veteran can get care nearer home and family; and a State Veterans Home, which VA.gov describes as owned and managed by the state.

VA’s Community Living Center page is direct: “A Community Living Center (CLC) is a VA Nursing Home.” It offers nursing-home-level care, including help with activities of daily living and 24-hour skilled nursing care, and the page says a CLC “resembles ‘home’ as much as possible,” with family-friendly places for visiting, rooms veterans are invited to decorate, and pets allowed to visit or live there.

For a Community Living Center or a community nursing home, VA’s long-term care overview says VA provides the care “IF you meet certain eligibility criteria involving your service-connected status, level of disability, and income.” The Community Living Center page adds: “You must first be enrolled in the VA health system, and be medically and psychiatrically stable,” and it says eligibility also depends on clinical need and whether a place is available. None of this guarantees a particular setting. State Veterans Homes are run by the states; Illinois’s are covered below.

Knowing which settings VA can provide is a different question from knowing which kind of care your veteran parent needs now: help at home, a respite stay, assisted living or a nursing home.

Respite care: a break for the family caregiver

VA.gov’s long-term care page says a veteran “may be able to get a service called respite care as part of your VA healthcare benefits.” It pays for a person to come into the home, or for the veteran to go to a programme outside the home, while the family caregiver takes a break. VA’s Geriatrics and Extended Care page says the programme is for veterans who need help with activities of daily living, and also for veterans who are isolated or whose caregiver is experiencing burden.

It comes in two forms. Home Respite Care “pays for a person to come to a Veteran’s home,” or for the veteran to attend an adult day health care programme while the family caregiver takes a break or runs errands. Nursing Home Respite Care “pays for a Veteran to go to a nursing home” while the family is away for a few days, and VA.gov notes: “This type of respite should be scheduled in advance.”

VA.gov’s eligibility rule: “All enrolled Veterans are eligible for Respite Care IF they meet the clinical criteria for the service and it is available.” Services vary by location, and if the respite is provided by a community agency, an adult day health care centre or a nursing home rather than by VA itself, the veteran also needs to meet community care eligibility.

A copay may apply: “A copay for Respite Care may be charged based on your VA service-connected disability status and financial information.” Neither VA.gov page this guide read states an annual day limit for Respite Care, so this guide does not publish one. Ask your VA social worker or case manager what is available near your parent.

What VA will not pay for: room and board in assisted living

This is the limit that catches families off guard. VA’s long-term care overview says: “The VA does not pay for room and board in residential settings such as Assisted Living or Adult Family Homes.” It adds that residential settings and nursing homes each have their own eligibility requirements, and that a veteran may still receive some of VA’s home and community services while living in a residential setting.

VA.gov’s newer long-term care page says of these settings: “These places are not run, staffed, or paid for by VA, but we do inspect and approve each one.” VA benefits may still help pay for extra services there, such as visits from a VA health care provider.

This is where the Aid and Attendance rate of the VA pension comes in for veterans and surviving spouses who qualify: it is money a family can put toward care costs such as the cost of assisted living. As that guide explains, it is a contribution toward care costs, not a programme that pays for assisted living.

Help at home that VA names

VA’s long-term care overview lists Geriatric Evaluation, Adult Day Health Care, Homemaker and Home Health Aide Care, Respite Care and Skilled Home Health Care, and says: “All enrolled Veterans are eligible for these services.” To get one, the veteran must have a clinical need for it and it must be available where they live.

VA.gov’s newer page describes several of them. Adult Day Health Care is a programme a veteran can go to during the day for “social activities, companionship, and recreation” as well as care and support. A homemaker or home health aide is “A trained caregiver (supervised by a registered nurse) who comes into your home to help you care for yourself.” Skilled home health care sends providers from a community-based home health agency to the house for care such as wound care, physical therapy, social work support and help with daily tasks. Home-Based Primary Care is “A VA healthcare team, led by a VA doctor, who provides services” such as nursing care and physical therapy in the home.

What it costs

VA’s long-term care overview states: “Long term care copays are not charged until the 22nd day of care.” After that, the Community Living Center page says “A copay may be charged for CLC care based on your VA service-connected disability status and financial information,” and the application described below is what sets the amount.

Hospice care is treated differently: “Copays are NOT charged for Hospice Care provided in any setting.” For services VA health care does not cover, VA.gov’s newer page says families may be able to pay through Medicaid, Medicare or private insurance.

This guide has not verified how a VA pension interacts with a Community Living Center copay. That depends on income and disability rules only VA can apply to one person’s file, so ask the VA social worker handling the application.

How to apply

VA.gov gives the same instruction for Community Living Center care and for Respite Care: contact your VA social worker or case manager to complete VA Form 10-10EC, Application for Extended Care Benefits, which also tells you the amount of your copay. The veteran must already be enrolled in VA health care, and VA’s overview warns: “Receiving financial compensation for a VA disability does NOT automatically enroll you in VA health care.”

Illinois runs five State Veterans Homes

The Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs lists five Illinois Veterans’ Homes, in Anna, Chicago, LaSalle, Manteno and Quincy, serving “Illinois veterans and eligible spouses seeking skilled and domiciliary care.” It says the Anna and Quincy homes offer care to qualifying spouses. The Illinois Department of Public Health states: “Each Home is licensed by IDPH and certified by the US Department of Veterans Affairs Medical Center of jurisdiction.”

IDPH also describes a protection under Public Act 100-0632: if two or more residents of an Illinois Veterans Home are diagnosed with an infectious disease within one month or less, the home must, within 24 hours of being notified of the second diagnosis, notify each resident and their emergency contact or next of kin in writing, post a notice near its main entrance, and notify the Department of Veterans’ Affairs and the Department of Public Health.

This guide has not verified the homes’ costs or admission rules. The Illinois Department of Veterans’ Affairs publishes an admission application for the homes; contact it directly before relying on a figure found elsewhere.

Where this fits with the rest of this site

This site covers three VA paths, and they are not interchangeable. Aid and Attendance is an increased monthly rate of the VA pension for veterans and some surviving spouses who already qualify for a pension. Veteran Directed Care gives a veteran who qualifies a budget to hire their own help at home. This guide is the third: care VA provides or pays for itself, under its own eligibility rules rather than a pension calculation.

If the open question in your family is which level of care your parent needs, rather than which VA programme pays for it, the free six-question assessment on this site walks through that separately. It asks for no name, no email and no phone number, and your answers never leave your device.
