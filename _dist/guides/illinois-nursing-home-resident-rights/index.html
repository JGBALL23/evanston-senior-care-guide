What rights does your parent have in an Illinois nursing home or care facility?

Short answer

Illinois residents of nursing homes and other long-term care facilities keep legal rights that the facility cannot override: a say in their own care, privacy, control of their own money, a written contract, and protection from being discharged except for one of four stated reasons. The Illinois Department on Aging sets these out in a brochure called Residents’ Rights for People in Long-Term Care Facilities. It is a summary, not the full statute — the brochure itself says to ask your ombudsman for the complete list.

Where this list comes from

Every nursing home and long-term care facility resident in Illinois keeps rights the facility cannot take away simply because they now live there. The Illinois Department on Aging publishes a brochure, Residents’ Rights for People in Long-Term Care Facilities, that sets out what those rights are in plain language, grouped under headings such as safety and good care, participating in your own care, privacy, money management, contract information, Medicaid and Medicare information, and the right to stay in the facility. This guide reports what that document says, heading by heading.

One limit stated plainly on the brochure's own back page: “For a full list of rights, talk to your ombudsman.” This guide will not claim to be the complete statutory list — it reports what the Department on Aging's own summary document says, and no more.

Knowing what rights your parent has on paper is a different question from knowing whether the facility they are in is still the right level of care for them.

Safety, dignity and a say in your own care

The brochure states a resident “must not be abused by anyone — physically, verbally, mentally, financially, or sexually.” On care itself, the facility “must develop a written care plan that states all the services it provides” and “must make reasonable arrangements to meet your needs and choices.” A resident has the right to choose their own doctor, to receive information about their medical condition and treatment in a language they understand, and to see their medical records within 24 hours of asking. The brochure also lists the right to make a Durable Power of Attorney for Health Care, a Living Will, a Declaration for Mental Health, and a Do Not Resuscitate order.

On privacy, the brochure says medical and personal care information is private, staff must knock before entering a resident's room, and the facility may not share information about a resident without permission. Visits and phone calls are private unless a doctor has ordered limited visits for medical reasons, and a married couple has the right to share a room if one is available.

Money, contracts and what the facility cannot do with either

A resident has the right to manage their own money, and the brochure says the facility “may not become your money manager nor your Social Security representative payee without your permission.” A resident may see their own financial record at any time, and the facility “must give you an itemized statement at least once every 3 months.”

On contracts, a resident must be given a document stating what services are provided and how much they cost, and the facility “must not require anyone else to sign an agreement stating they will pay for your bill unless they are your court appointed guardian.” On Medicaid and Medicare, a resident has the right to apply for either, the facility must give information on how to apply — including for spousal impoverishment protections — and, the brochure states, “if you receive Medicaid, the facility cannot make you pay for anything for which Medicaid pays.”

Being asked to leave: the only four reasons, and the right to appeal

The brochure states a resident has the right to keep living in their facility, and that it “can force you to leave” only because the bill has not been paid, the resident is dangerous to themselves or others, their medical needs cannot be met there, or the facility closes. A written notice is required, and it must state why the resident is being asked to move, how to appeal, and include a self-addressed envelope to the Illinois Department of Public Health. A resident has the right to appeal a discharge within 10 days, and the brochure directs anyone needing help with an involuntary discharge notice to call the Illinois Department on Aging at 1-800-252-8966.

On returning after a hospital stay, the brochure says that in most cases a Medicaid resident must be allowed to return to the facility after a hospital stay, even with a written discharge notice already issued, and that a facility must hold a bed for a resident hospitalized more than 10 days once one becomes available. A private-pay resident, the brochure says, must be told in writing what they need to do to hold their bed while hospitalized. A resident also has the right to be told in advance if their room or roommate is changing.

Rights that do not disappear at the door

The brochure is explicit that a resident keeps their rights as a citizen of Illinois and the United States. It lists the right to vote, to take part in social and community activities, to participate in the facility's Resident Council, and to meet with the Long-Term Care Ombudsman, community organisations, legal advocates and members of the public who visit. A resident has the right to present grievances and get a prompt response, and the brochure states the facility “may not threaten or punish you in any way for asserting your rights or presenting grievances.”

If a right is not being respected

The brochure's own suggested order is: write down what happened, with dates, times and who was involved; raise it with facility staff through the grievance procedure; bring it to the Resident Council; and contact the Illinois Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program, which the Department on Aging describes elsewhere as a free, confidential, resident-directed advocate — reach it through the Senior HelpLine at 1-800-252-8966 (711 TRS). The brochure separately names the Central Complaint Registry, 800-252-4343, as the Illinois Department of Public Health's channel for a formal complaint. A full walk-through of both channels, including hours and how to file in writing, is in How to report a problem at an Illinois care facility.

One Evanston-specific detail from the brochure's own contact page: the Ombudsman region covering the city lists a direct number for City of Evanston: 1-847-448-8696, separate from the number listed for the rest of that region.
