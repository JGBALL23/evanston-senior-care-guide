Can a veteran get paid in-home care through the VA?

Short answer

Sometimes, through a programme called Veteran Directed Care, which is separate from the VA pension this site covers elsewhere. VA.gov describes it as giving “Veterans of all ages the opportunity to receive the Home and Community Based Services they need in a consumer-directed way.” A veteran who qualifies gets a budget and help building a spending plan, then hires their own workers — who can include a family member or a neighbour — rather than being assigned an agency worker. It depends on clinical need, enrollment in VA health care, and whether the local VA offers it at all.

What Veteran Directed Care actually is

This is easy to miss because it sits inside VA.gov’s Geriatrics and Extended Care pages rather than beside the pension material most families find first. VA.gov’s own description: Veteran Directed Care “gives Veterans of all ages the opportunity to receive the Home and Community Based Services” they need “in a consumer-directed way.”

“Consumer-directed” is the operative word. Instead of VA (or an agency VA contracts with) choosing and scheduling an aide, the veteran or their representative receives help developing a spending plan, then hires workers themselves — and VA.gov is explicit that this can include “their own family member or neighbor” — to help the veteran live independently in the community.

VA.gov lists examples of the daily activities the hired help can cover: eating, getting dressed, personal grooming, bathing, using the bathroom, moving from one place to another, and grocery shopping. It also names who the programme is aimed at: veterans who need personal care and help with daily activities, and veterans who are isolated or whose caregiver is under strain.

Knowing that the VA will fund help at home answers how your veteran parent might pay for it. It does not answer whether help at home, rather than a higher level of care, is actually enough.

Who qualifies, in VA.gov's own words

VA.gov states the rule in one sentence: “All enrolled Veterans are eligible for Veteran Directed Care if they are eligible for community care,” “meet the clinical criteria for the service, and it is available” where they live. That is three separate gates, and the page is explicit that services vary by location — this is not a benefit every VA medical centre necessarily offers.

The Veteran Directed Care page does not itself spell out what “eligible for community care” means, so this guide read VA.gov's own community care eligibility page rather than guess. That page sets two basic requirements that must both be true — “you're enrolled in or eligible for VA health care” and “you have approval from your VA health care team before you get care from a community provider” — plus at least one of several further conditions, such as needing a service VA does not provide at any facility or living somewhere without a full-service VA facility nearby.

This guide has not confirmed, on any VA.gov page, that the general community-care eligibility rules apply to Veteran Directed Care exactly as written for an outside specialist referral — the Veteran Directed Care page simply says “eligible for community care” without cross-linking the detail. Rather than assume the two pages mean exactly the same thing in every case, ask a VA social worker whether your veteran parent clears this gate before ruling the programme in or out.

This is not Aid and Attendance

Families researching VA help with senior care usually find VA Aid and Attendance first, and it is easy to assume Veteran Directed Care is the same thing under another name. It is not.

Aid and Attendance is an increased rate added to the Veterans Pension or Survivors Pension — a cash benefit, administered on the benefits side of VA, that a family can put toward care in any setting. Veteran Directed Care is a health care service, administered through VA's geriatrics and extended-care programme, that pays for a defined package of in-home help rather than handing the veteran cash. One pays a monthly amount against the veteran's own countable income; the other funds a spending plan for specific hired help. This guide found no VA.gov page confirming whether a veteran can draw on both at once, so it will not guess — ask a VA social worker about your specific situation.

How to start

VA.gov does not describe a separate application form for Veteran Directed Care itself. What it says instead is to use the Veteran Decision Aid for Care at Home or in the Community “to help you figure out what home care services or long term care services may best meet your needs” now or in the future, and then: “If Veteran Directed Care seems right for you, talk with your VA social worker and find out if it is available” in your location.

A veteran who is not yet enrolled in VA health care has to clear that step first, since enrollment is one of the stated gates above. VA.gov lists four ways to apply for VA health care: online, by phone at 877-222-8387 (Monday–Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. ET), by mailing a signed VA Form 10-10EZ to the Health Eligibility Center, PO Box 5207, Janesville, WI 53547-5207, or in person at the nearest VA medical centre or clinic with the same signed form in hand.

A figure this guide will not guess at

Families will want to know the size of the budget a veteran actually receives. This guide looked for that figure on VA.gov and did not find a published national amount — the page describes the spending plan as based on an individual assessment, not a fixed rate. Anything quoting a specific dollar figure for Veteran Directed Care is not working from a VA.gov page this guide could locate. Ask the VA social worker handling the case what the assessed budget would actually be.
