Does Medicare pay for a home health aide or nurse at home?

Short answer

Only for skilled care, and only while your parent is homebound. Medicare pays $0 for a covered home health visit from a Medicare-certified agency, but it will only start one when a doctor who has examined your parent in person orders intermittent, skilled nursing or therapy and confirms your parent is homebound. A home health aide for bathing, feeding or help walking is covered only alongside that skilled care — Medicare does not pay for an aide, or for ongoing personal care, by itself.

What the home health benefit actually covers

Medicare.gov describes home health as a defined set of services delivered at home by a Medicare-certified home health agency, not a general-purpose way to pay for help at home. What is covered:

  • Skilled nursing care — “medically necessary part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care,” such as wound care, injections, IV or nutrition therapy, and monitoring an unstable health condition
  • Physical, occupational and speech-language therapy — if certain conditions are met
  • Medical social services
  • Home health aide services — part-time or intermittent, and only while you are also receiving skilled nursing or therapy. Examples given are bathing, feeding and help with walking
  • Injectable osteoporosis drugs for women who meet certain criteria
  • Durable medical equipment and medical supplies for use at home

Read that aide line again: it is covered only while skilled nursing, physical therapy, speech-language pathology or occupational therapy is also happening. The next two sections explain why that single word decides so much of what follows.

Qualifying for Medicare’s home health benefit turns on a doctor’s definition of “skilled” and “homebound” — not on whether your parent is safe to leave alone, which is usually the real question a family is asking.

The test that decides it: skilled need and homebound, both at once

Medicare.gov sets out conditions that all have to hold before a home health visit is covered:

  • A skilled need. Your parent needs part-time or intermittent skilled nursing or therapy — not an ongoing, open-ended need for help
  • Homebound status, both parts at once. Leaving home is not recommended, or your parent has trouble leaving without help, because of an illness or injury; and your parent is normally unable to leave home, and leaving takes a considerable and taxing effort. Medical treatment and short, infrequent non-medical absences — the page gives religious services as an example — are allowed without losing homebound status, and so is adult day care
  • A face-to-face encounter. “A doctor or other provider (like a nurse practitioner) must see you in person and confirm you need home health care” — this cannot be done by chart review alone
  • A doctor’s order, carried out by a Medicare-certified home health agency

Occupational therapy has its own rule worth knowing: it “must first be ordered along with nursing, physical therapy, or speech-language pathology” — but once that has happened, it “can continue on its own” even after the service that started it ends.

What it has never covered, however much the need

Medicare.gov lists this explicitly as not covered by the home health benefit:

  • 24-hour-a-day care at home
  • Home meal delivery
  • Homemaker services — shopping, cleaning — that are unrelated to the care plan
  • Custodial or personal care, such as help with bathing or dressing, when that is the only care needed

That last line is the one families run into. A home health aide is part of this benefit only as long as skilled nursing or therapy is also underway. The moment the skilled need ends — a wound heals, a therapy goal is met — the aide visits built around it generally end too, even if the bathing or dressing help your parent needs has not gone anywhere. Ongoing, non-medical personal care at home in Illinois is a different programme with a different eligibility test: see Can my parent get paid help at home in Illinois?

What it costs

Medicare.gov states the cost of the core benefit in five words: “You pay nothing for covered home health services.” That is $0 coinsurance and $0 copayment for a qualifying visit — not a reduced rate.

The exception is equipment, not care. For Medicare-covered durable medical equipment supplied as part of home health, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after you meet the Part B deductible, which is $283 for 2026.

Before giving you anything outside the covered benefit, the agency is required to tell you — verbally and in writing, in advance — what Medicare will not pay for, using a notice called an Advance Beneficiary Notice. Ask for it in writing before agreeing to anything the agency describes as extra.

How much care, and how often

If your parent qualifies, Medicare.gov says visits are unlimited — there is no cap on the number of home health visits over time, only on the intensity of any single period of care.

That intensity cap: skilled nursing and home health aide services combined generally run up to 8 hours a day and 28 hours a week. A short-term increase — under 8 hours a day, up to 35 hours a week — is allowed if your parent’s provider decides it is necessary. Both figures explain the line above about 24-hour care: this benefit is built around scheduled, intermittent visits, not a presence in the home around the clock, at any funding level.
