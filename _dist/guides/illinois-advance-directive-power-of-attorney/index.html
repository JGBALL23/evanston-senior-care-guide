Does my parent need a power of attorney for health care in Illinois?

Short answer

No Illinois law requires one, but if your parent never signs a Power of Attorney for Health Care, Illinois law still appoints someone to decide for them — in a fixed order you do not get to pick, running from a court-appointed guardian through a spouse, adult children, parents, siblings and grandchildren before it ever reaches a close friend. A Power of Attorney for Health Care lets your parent name that person themselves, while they still can.

The four advance directives Illinois recognises

The Illinois Department of Public Health describes four separate documents, each answering a different question, and each with its own state law behind it:

  • Power of Attorney for Health Care — names an agent to make health care decisions when your parent cannot (Illinois Power of Attorney Act)
  • Living Will Declaration — states, in advance, whether to withhold or withdraw death-delaying procedures if your parent has a terminal condition and cannot say so themselves (Illinois Living Will Act, 755 ILCS 35/1)
  • Mental Health Treatment Preference Declaration — records consent to, or refusal of, specific mental health treatments in advance (Mental Health Treatment Preference Declaration Act, 755 ILCS 43/10)
  • Practitioner Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST) — a medical order, signed by a doctor as well as your parent, setting out which life-sustaining treatments to use or withhold (Health Care Surrogate Act)

None of the four is required. The Illinois Department of Public Health states plainly that no facility, health care professional or insurer can require your parent to execute any of them as a condition of receiving treatment or insurance: “It is entirely your decision.”

What a Power of Attorney for Health Care actually does

Your parent (the principal) names an agent to make personal and health care decisions on their behalf, under the Illinois Power of Attorney Act. The Illinois Department of Public Health sets out what that agent can and cannot do:

  • The agent must follow your parent’s specific instructions, including on life-sustaining treatment and anatomical gifts
  • The agent cannot be your parent’s own health care professional or other health care provider
  • Unless your parent sets a time limit, the power lasts from signing until death
  • Your parent can name a backup agent, and can cancel the document at any time — changes must be made in writing
  • Someone other than the agent should witness the signing
  • Illinois accepts an electronic copy if the signed original is unavailable

Free forms — a “Power of Attorney for Health Care” document and a Spanish-language version — are published by both the Illinois Department of Public Health and the Illinois Department on Aging.

If there is no Power of Attorney, Illinois still picks someone — just not who your family might choose

This is the part most families never learn until they need it. If your parent has no Power of Attorney for Health Care and loses the ability to decide, Illinois does not leave the decision to whichever relative is in the room. Under the Health Care Surrogate Act, two doctors must first certify that your parent cannot make their own decisions. Only then is a surrogate decision-maker identified — and the law picks that person from a fixed order, not from family agreement:

  1. Guardian of the person
  2. Spouse
  3. Adult children
  4. Either parent
  5. Adult siblings
  6. Adult grandchildren
  7. A close friend
  8. Guardian of the estate

That order has real limits built into it, too. A surrogate who is not a court-appointed guardian cannot direct the withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment unless two doctors certify a qualifying condition — a terminal condition, permanent unconsciousness, or an incurable or irreversible condition. The same surrogate also cannot consent to electroconvulsive treatment, psychotropic medication, or admission to a mental health facility, without a court’s approval. A Power of Attorney for Health Care is what lets your parent choose their own decision-maker instead of relying on this default order, and lets that chosen agent act without those extra restrictions.

The living will and the POLST answer narrower questions

A Living Will only applies if your parent has a terminal condition and cannot state their own wishes — it does nothing before then. Illinois requires two witnesses, neither of whom can be your parent’s own health care professional, and the Illinois Department of Public Health states that food and water cannot be withdrawn under a living will if doing so would be the only cause of death. It also does not take effect if your parent is pregnant and the treating provider believes a live birth is possible. If your parent has both a living will and a Power of Attorney for Health Care, the named agent decides — the living will only governs if that agent is unavailable.

A POLST is different in kind, not just in name: it is a medical order, not only a statement of wishes. It needs your parent’s signature (or an authorised representative’s) and the attending practitioner’s, and the Illinois Department of Public Health states it will not be entered into the medical record without every required signature. It can record, among other things, that cardiopulmonary resuscitation should not be attempted if your parent’s heart or breathing stops.

What a nursing home or hospital must do — and must never do

Under the federal Patient Self-Determination Act, Medicare and Medicaid providers — including nursing facilities — must tell your parent about the right to make an advance directive, in the Illinois Department of Public Health’s words, “upon admission to a facility or when provision of care begins.” No facility can condition treatment on actually signing one.

If a facility objects to following your parent’s directive on moral or religious grounds, the Illinois Department of Public Health states it must say so and must keep providing care until your parent can be transferred to a provider who will follow it. It cannot simply stop caring for your parent while that objection stands.

Where to get the forms, and free help filling them out

The Illinois Department on Aging and the Illinois Department of Public Health both publish free forms for a Power of Attorney for Health Care, a Living Will, a Mental Health Treatment Preference Declaration and a POLST. The POLST form is available in English, Arabic, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, French, Hindi, Korean, Polish and Spanish.

Filling one out does not have to mean hiring a lawyer. The Illinois Department on Aging funds local Area Agencies on Aging to contract with legal aid representatives who, in the Department’s own words, “may help with a wide variety of civil law issues.” Find the agency covering Evanston through the Department’s AAA map tool or its provider Service Locator, or call the Senior HelpLine on 1-800-252-8966 (711 TRS) to ask where to start.
